## 4. Why These Quiz Turns Work In The App

The three primary targets all clear the v2 app's inline-quiz bar:

- **One flagged turn, one quiz.** Each of t23, t32, t55 contains exactly one inferential move visible in its own text, so the quiz attaches cleanly at one turn_id without needing surrounding context to be pulled forward.
- **Short direct prompts are possible.** For each, a one-sentence question like "what is James doing here?" or "what step did James skip?" works without quoting the turn. The turn itself is rendered on screen adjacent to the quiz, so restating would be redundant.
- **Honest distractors are available.** Each turn supports at least three tempting wrong options drawn from the canonical flaw set. The distractors do not rely on invented flaw-adjacent concepts.
- **Feedback can stay short.** Because the missing step is nameable in one plain sentence ("he jumped from a Louisiana river to Memphis tap water"), correct/incorrect feedback does not have to compensate for muddy source text.
- **Distinct scenes.** Students encounter the three bands at three different narrative moments, so the quiz cadence mirrors the emotional arc — wound up, widening, quieter — rather than clustering.
- **No analytical gymnastics required from the package builder.** Nothing in the transcript forces the author into abstract wording or contrived distractors.

## 5. Why These Flaws Are Visible To 6th Graders

### t23 — the one-breath chain

A 6th grader can literally count the "and/so/which" links. The taxonomy's "four or more so→so→so in a single breath" cue is satisfied by the turn's own shape. A student trained on the flaw cards can recite back: "he went from red eyes to 'it has to be the water' in one breath — that's the chain." The base observation (red eyes, a TikTok about Louisiana) and the conclusion (Memphis water is toxic) are in the same scene, so the gap is visible on the page.

### t32 — the ducks leap

"The ducks are in the pond, so the ducks are next" is a one-step structure a 6th grader can paraphrase. The missing step — "but you haven't shown the pond is toxic, and pond water is not tap water" — is nameable in plain language. The "so" bridge is a visible signal phrase the student can point to without reading between lines.

### t55 — the quiet re-run

This is the hardest, but still visible at 6th-grade level after brief instruction, because Leela just disproved the water theory in the immediately prior turns. A student can feel the mismatch: "Leela just said it's not the water, and he's still saying something's getting into the fur." The instructor-friendly paraphrase is available: "the fur looks wrong, so something is getting into it" skips the step that would explain why "looks wrong" implies "something is getting into it." This is exactly the heightened cue — reads as reasonable until paraphrased back.

### t20 — the bio-line source cue

A 6th grader, after practicing trusting-a-source-too-quickly, can recognize "his bio literally says he covers pollution stuff" as a credential-adjacent cue, not a credential. This is not an easy catch, which is correct for heightened.
