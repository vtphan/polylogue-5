# Simplified Flaw Taxonomy

This document defines the starter reasoning-flaw taxonomy for the simplified Lens framework.

It is intentionally narrow.

Its purpose is not to capture every interesting reasoning pattern.

Its purpose is to support:

- beginner-friendly instruction
- clear episode design
- obvious teachable moments
- simple downstream app interactions

## 1. Taxonomy Design Principle

Only include flaws that are:

- obvious enough to feel like real flaws
- recognizable in ordinary dialogue
- explainable in plain language
- usable in multiple-choice teaching interactions

If a candidate flaw is too subtle, too technical, or too dependent on expert interpretation, it should not be part of the starter taxonomy.

## 2. Starter Flaw Set

The simplified framework should begin with these five reasoning flaws:

1. jumping to a conclusion
2. not enough evidence
3. ignoring another perspective
4. trusting a source too quickly
5. missing important conditions or consequences

These are the current student-facing categories.

## 3. Flaw Definitions

## 3.1 Jumping to a Conclusion

### Student-facing label

`jumping to a conclusion`

### Plain definition

The speaker moves from one idea or observation to a bigger conclusion too quickly.

### What it sounds like in dialogue

- "So that means..."
- "It has to be..."
- "Obviously..."
- "That proves..."

### Why it is useful

This flaw is common, visible, and easy to teach.

It is a strong lower-level category.

## 3.2 Not Enough Evidence

### Student-facing label

`not enough evidence`

### Plain definition

The speaker is making a claim without enough support.

### What it sounds like in dialogue

- one example used to support a big claim
- one sighting or one story treated like proof
- confidence that grows faster than the evidence

### Why it is useful

This is one of the clearest critical-thinking lessons students can practice.

It is also highly reusable across episodes.

## 3.3 Ignoring Another Perspective

### Student-facing label

`ignoring another perspective`

### Plain definition

The speaker is not considering another person's point of view, another explanation, or another possibility that matters.

### What it sounds like in dialogue

- "What else could it be?"
- "Nothing else fits."
- only one side is treated as worth considering
- another affected person's view never enters the discussion

### Why it is useful

This flaw helps students learn that good reasoning includes checking what might be missing from the frame.

For beginner levels, it should be used only when the missing perspective or alternative is clear.

## 3.4 Trusting a Source Too Quickly

### Student-facing label

`trusting a source too quickly`

### Plain definition

The speaker treats a source as reliable without checking who it is, how they know, or whether they can be trusted.

### What it sounds like in dialogue

- "I saw it online..."
- "Someone said..."
- "They're a researcher, so..."
- "The comments said..."

### Why it is useful

This flaw is easy to teach because it turns source evaluation into a concrete question:

- why should we trust this source?

## 3.5 Missing Important Conditions or Consequences

### Student-facing label

`missing important conditions or consequences`

### Plain definition

The speaker leaves out an important detail about when the claim would hold, or what might happen if they are wrong.

### What it sounds like in dialogue

- assuming something is always true
- ignoring the situation or limits
- ignoring who might be affected
- acting like a conclusion works in every case

### Why it is useful

This flaw is slightly harder than the others, but still useful if written clearly.

For lower levels, it should be used only when the missing condition or consequence is obvious.

## 4. Difficulty Guidance

Not all flaws are equally easy to teach.

Recommended lower-level emphasis:

- jumping to a conclusion
- not enough evidence
- trusting a source too quickly

Recommended middle-level use:

- ignoring another perspective
- missing important conditions or consequences

This does not mean the last two are forbidden at lower levels.

It means they should be used only when the flaw is especially visible.

## 5. What Is Not in the Starter Taxonomy

The starter taxonomy should exclude categories like:

- internal consistency
- reasoning completeness
- relevance
- overgeneralization as a separate category
- condition sensitivity as a technical label
- source diversity as a separate category
- cognitive bias labels
- group-dynamics labels

These may still matter upstream, but they should not be part of the first student-facing taxonomy.

## 6. Mapping from Older Framework Concepts

The simplified taxonomy can still absorb older concepts underneath.

Examples:

- `inferential_validity` often maps to `jumping to a conclusion`
- `sufficiency` often maps to `not enough evidence`
- `perspective_engagement` often maps to `ignoring another perspective`
- `source_credibility` maps to `trusting a source too quickly`
- `condition_sensitivity` and `consequence_consideration` often map to `missing important conditions or consequences`

These mappings are approximate and should be used as authoring guidance, not as a rigid conversion rule.

## 7. Authoring Rule

When generating or reviewing an episode:

- choose the simplest valid flaw label
- prefer plain language over technical precision
- avoid using multiple flaw labels for the same teachable turn unless there is a strong reason

If a turn could fit several categories, choose the one that would be easiest for a beginner to understand.

## 8. Validation Questions

For each designated flaw moment, ask:

1. Would an ordinary reader recognize this as a flaw?
2. Can it be labeled with one of the five starter flaws?
3. Can it be explained in one or two short sentences?
4. Would this work in a multiple-choice level?
5. Is this too subtle for the intended difficulty?

If the answer to several of these is no, the flaw moment should be revised or rejected.

## 9. Future Expansion

The taxonomy can expand later.

But the first version should stay intentionally small until the pipeline and app prove they can teach these categories clearly and consistently.
