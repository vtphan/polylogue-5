# Story Design

This document is operator guidance for designing a Polylogue story under the **episodes-first authoring model** (Part 13 of `framework/docs/story-pipeline-revision.md`). It is written for the person sitting at the keyboard authoring a story design doc and per-episode drafts, and the curriculum coordinator reading over their shoulder. It is not a schema reference and not a pipeline runbook — for those, see Appendix B of `story-pipeline-revision.md` and `framework/docs/operator-manual.md`.

A story is the unit of curriculum the pipeline produces. The system enforces structural requirements (cast bounds via prose review, coverage contract via the validator, mixed-valence rotation via the validator). The creative design has narrative requirements that no validator can capture. Both matter. A story that satisfies the validator but bores 6th graders has failed; a story that excites them but leaves the framework's inventory uncovered has also failed. This document is about getting both right.

## What a Polylogue story is

**Story.** A self-contained multi-episode narrative with a fixed cast, an arc across episodes, and a declared coverage contract. A story is captured in two kinds of artifact, both authored as prose:

- **The story design doc** at `framework/stories/{story_id}.md`. Markdown with YAML frontmatter at the top (`story_id`, `title`, `coverage_mode`, `declared_facets`, `declared_cognitive_patterns`, `declared_social_dynamics`, `episode_count`) plus a prose body (premise, setting, cast, arc summary, stakes, pedagogical commitments). The frontmatter is the machine-readable contract; the prose body is the source of truth for character identity.
- **The per-episode drafts** at `framework/stories/{story_id}/episode_{NN}.md`, one per episode. Each draft has YAML frontmatter (the operator's authoring artifact for one episode — see Appendix B of `framework/docs/story-pipeline-revision.md`) plus a prose body (beats, authorial notes, why-these-targets).

**Episode.** One discussion within a story. Each episode is generated by the per-episode pipeline (`/create_episode` → `/create_transcript` → `/analyze_transcript` → app downstream) and lives at `artifacts/{story_id}/episodes/episode_{NN}/`. The shared episode plan is `episode.yaml`.

**Cast.** The recurring characters defined in the story design doc's prose. Each character is described in narrative terms — voice, the way they reason, their lens disposition, and any growth arc they have. There is no separate cast schema; the design doc IS the cast definition.

**Session.** One classroom sitting on one episode. Unchanged by the story redesign.

The single most important thing to internalize is the **design inversion**. The legacy system asked: pick the facets you want to teach, then invent personas that will display them. The story system asks the opposite: design a cast you find interesting, plan an arc that surfaces their tendencies in different episodes, then check that the cast collectively covers what the framework requires. The cast is what makes the stories interesting; the cast's engineered distribution of weaknesses and strengths across episodes is what guarantees coverage. These two requirements are no longer in tension because they are no longer separate steps.

## The cast: six design rules

These rules are load-bearing — `story_consistency_reviewer` will check them as it reads the design doc and per-episode drafts together — but they also encode design wisdom you should believe in, not merely satisfy.

**1. The cast collectively carries the declared coverage.** Not in every episode — somewhere in the season. A facet can appear as a target weakness in episode 2 and as a target strength in episode 4. A cognitive pattern can surface in one character's episode and never again. What is forbidden is declaring coverage of something the cast cannot plausibly carry.

**2. No character is an embodied fallacy.** Each character carries 2–3 cognitive tendencies and contributes to one social dynamic. Tendencies surface or stay dormant depending on the situation. A character whose only function is "she demonstrates confirmation_bias" is decoration, not a person, and 6th graders will see through it. Real people have several flaws and several strengths and the situation determines which ones show up.

**3. Each character has at least one stable strength they reliably bring.** Strengths are not consolation prizes for weak characters. They are how the cast teaches that good reasoning is a practice everyone is capable of, not a property of one student.

**4. At most two characters in the cast have visible growth arcs.** Constancy is the default; growth is the exception. Two reasons. First, growth is dramatically expensive — a character whose tendencies change across episodes requires more episode-aware student attention. Second, students need stable reference points. If everyone is growing, no one is recognizable. Pick the two characters whose growth carries the most narrative weight and let the rest be themselves throughout.

**5. The cast collectively models the three lens dispositions.** Logic-leaning, Evidence-leaning, Scope-leaning. None of these labels ever appear in dialog, but the cast should *embody* them: the kid who wants the argument to be airtight, the kid who wants the data, the kid who asks who is missing from the conversation. A cast where everyone reasons the same way cannot teach perspectival learning.

**6. Cast size is bounded: 4–6 characters total, 2–3 leads per episode.** If a character can be removed without breaking the coverage check, remove them. For a 5-episode focused-coverage pilot, **five characters is the right number**, not six — six characters across 5 episodes with 2–3 leads each means several characters lead only one episode, which weakens the "recurring cast students recognize" rationale that the story model exists to deliver.

**Strength-carrier rotation.** In the legacy system, strength tended to live with whichever persona was about to lose. In the story system, strength carriers rotate explicitly across episodes. `validate_story.py` computes per-character strength counts and fails the story if any one character carries more than half the strengths. The same rotation rule applies to weaknesses. This is the explicit answer to the legacy regression where the same persona always "won" the reasoning argument and always "lost" the social one.

## Cast prose and growth in the new model

Cast tendencies live in **prose** in the story design doc. Each character gets a section that describes how they reason: their voice, the cognitive moves they tend to make, the social dynamic they tend to drive or absorb, and (if they have a growth arc) the inflection that shifts how they reason mid-story.

There are no episode-indexed tendency fields and no `signal_template` machinery. Those existed in an earlier model that asked the operator to author the cast and the episodes as two separate machine-readable layers; the episodes-first revision deleted that layering. The cast is now defined in narrative; per-episode behavior is captured in the per-episode drafts; `story_consistency_reviewer` reads both as prose and checks that they agree.

Growth arcs are expressed in two places:
- **In the design doc's prose**, by describing the character before and after the inflection ("Sam starts the story keeping her head down to avoid disagreement; by the petition episode she's the one who refuses to let the conversation move on without a missing voice").
- **In the per-episode drafts**, by writing the pre-growth signals in episodes before the inflection and the post-growth signals in episodes after it. The inflection episode is the one where the shift happens.

Why constancy is the default: growth is expensive, both to author and for students to track. Cast voices are the things students learn to recognize. If a character's reasoning shifts every episode, there is no template to learn.

Why growth is the exception: stories without growth feel static. Two growing characters are enough to give the season an arc. More than two and the cast becomes hard to hold in mind.

## The coverage contract

The coverage contract is what ties story-level design to the framework's existing inventory. Without it, a story is a narrative artifact with vague pedagogical claims. With it, a story is auditable curriculum.

**Two modes.** A story declares either `full` or `focused` coverage in the design doc's YAML frontmatter.

- **Full coverage** commits the story to covering all 10 facets, all 8 cognitive patterns, and all 3 social dynamics across its episodes. This is the default ambition for stories of 6+ episodes. `validate_story.py` fails the story if any inventory entry is uncovered.
- **Focused coverage** lets the story declare a subset that the validator checks instead. This is for shorter mini-stories used in compact units. **The focused floor is hard:** a focused story must cover at least 3 facets, at least 1 cognitive pattern, and at least 1 social dynamic. Below that floor, focused becomes an escape hatch and the framework promise erodes.

For a focused story, the design doc frontmatter declares `declared_facets`, `declared_cognitive_patterns`, `declared_social_dynamics`. These are the only things the validator scores against. Everything outside the declared subset is unchecked.

**What `validate_story.py` checks.** The validator walks the design doc frontmatter and every per-episode draft frontmatter. Its results land in `framework/stories/validation/{story_id}-validation-report-{timestamp}.yaml` (gitignored; a fresh timestamped file is written on each run). None of the operator-authored files are mutated.

- *Coverage closure.* The union of (facet, pattern, dynamic) targets across all episode draft frontmatter must cover the declared subset (focused mode) or the full inventory (full mode). Every declared facet must appear as both a weakness and a strength somewhere in the season.
- *Lens distribution.* Each lens (`logic`, `evidence`, `scope`) must appear as a primary lens in at least one episode, and no single lens may be primary in more than half the episodes (rounded down). This prevents the legacy 5-scenario sequence's Scope-dominance regression while remaining feasible for short stories.
- *Mixed-valence shape rotation.* No single `mixed_valence_shape` value may appear in more than half of the episodes. The five shapes (`early_strength_collapse`, `strength_prevails`, `stalemate`, `self_correction`, `unresolved_disagreement`) are a menu, not a checklist.
- *Strength rotation* and *weakness rotation.* No single character carries more than half of the story's strengths or more than half of its weaknesses. Both are validator-computed from the per-episode draft `targets[]` and `strengths[]`, not operator-declared.
- *Hedged-annotation rule.* Every declared cognitive pattern and social dynamic must have at least one *unhedged* (single-label) annotation in some episode's `analysis.yaml`. This rule only runs after Phase 7 has produced analyses; Phase 6 authoring sees a "skipped" note. Hedged (multi-label) annotations are honest calibrations and are encouraged when the evidence underdetermines, but they do **not** satisfy story-level coverage.

The hedged-annotation rule is worth dwelling on, because it creates the right incentive across the whole pipeline. The evaluator commits to a single label when the evidence justifies it and hedges when it does not. Hedging is calibration, not coverage workaround. **The correct response to a coverage failure caused by persistent hedging is to revise the per-episode draft (or the design doc) so the evidence becomes unambiguous — not to pressure the evaluator to commit harder.** If the same pattern keeps coming back hedged across the season, that is information: the cast cannot actually carry that pattern in the situations the arc puts them in, and the design needs revision.

## What `story_consistency_reviewer` checks

`story_consistency_reviewer` is the prose-on-prose review counterpart to `validate_story.py`. It reads the story design doc and every per-episode draft (frontmatter and prose body) and checks character consistency, voice consistency, earned growth beats, and rubric items 1–8 from Part 10. Item 9 ("moment of surprise") stays human-only.

It is invoked routinely:
- After each new episode draft is authored, to catch character drift early.
- As a final pass before Phase 7 begins.
- Whenever a re-planning loop forces a draft revision in Phase 7, to check that the revision did not introduce drift.

The eight rubric items it scores:

1. Are the stakes concrete and personal to the cast?
2. Is the cast small enough (4–6) and distinct enough that each character has a predictable voice and a recognizable set of tendencies?
3. Does the arc have momentum across episodes — does each episode end with a reason to want the next?
4. Does the coverage contract close — can the cast actually carry every declared facet, pattern, and dynamic? *(also scored mechanically by `validate_story.py`)*
5. Is mixed-valence varied across episodes? *(also scored mechanically by `validate_story.py`)*
6. Does the ending earn its lack of tidy resolution?
7. Does any character feel like an embodied fallacy? If yes, redesign.
8. Would a 6th grader want to know what happens in episode 4 after reading episode 3?

A story that fails any of 1, 4, or 7 is not ready. The others are improvement targets.

**Item 9 is the one the agent will not check for you.** "Is there at least one moment of genuine surprise — a character doing something unexpected but in-character?" Surprise without violation of character is what teaches students that flaws are not traits and growth is not betrayal. You have to put this in by hand and you have to know when you have done it.

## What makes a Polylogue story excellent

The rubric above is the floor. Above the floor, a few things separate a serviceable story from one teachers will adopt and students will talk about:

- **Stakes that 11–13 year olds will actually care about.** Not abstract environmental harm. Something concrete, local, and personal: a place they love is threatened, a friend is being misjudged, a decision is about to be made that will affect their daily life.
- **A premise specific enough to feel real but general enough to travel.** "Saving our school's maker space" works in any school with a maker space. "Saving the Bridgewater Middle School maker space from Principal Vasquez" travels less well. Use generic place names and constructed school cultures.
- **Cast voices distinct enough that students can predict them.** By episode 3, students should be able to look at an unattributed line and guess which character said it more often than chance.
- **An arc with momentum.** Each episode ends with an unanswered question, an unresolved tension, or a piece of new information that recontextualizes what came before. Episode boundaries are commitments to come back, not stopping points.
- **At least one moment of genuine surprise per story** (rubric item 9, repeated here because it is the easiest item to forget).
- **An ending that resists tidy resolution.** Mixed-valence at the story level, not just the episode level. The kids do not unambiguously win. They learn something. They lose something. That is more honest than victory and more memorable than failure.

## A worked example: from cast prose to episode signal

The cast→episode→signal chain is the part of the design that most often goes wrong on a first attempt, because each link looks trivial in isolation and the overall consistency check is non-obvious. Here is one full pass.

Suppose the story design doc establishes **Maya** as a Logic-leaning kid who tends to overstate how settled a question is — she remembers a single source she encountered recently and treats it as the whole record. The design doc also notes that Maya is the one who states the group's emerging consensus a beat too early, making it hard to dissent, and that this is a habit she begins to soften from episode 4 onward (her growth arc).

Now you are authoring episode 2. Episode 2 targets `sufficiency` (a facet weakness) under the Evidence lens, with Maya as the carrier. In the per-episode draft frontmatter you write a target entry like:

```yaml
- facet: sufficiency
  lens: evidence
  carrier: Maya
  cognitive_pattern: false_certainty
  social_dynamic: group_pressure
  cognitive_signal: >
    Maya cites a single article she half-remembers from last week as if
    it settled the question of whether the city should fund the new bus
    route, and dismisses Jordan's request for more sources as overcautious.
  social_signal: >
    Maya states the consensus prematurely and Jordan acquiesces with a
    quiet "yeah, I guess" rather than pushing back on the missing sources.
```

That `cognitive_signal` is a faithful instantiation of how Maya reasons per the design doc — overstating settledness from one remembered source — **and** a concrete behavioral hook the dialog writer can stage in turn outline. `story_consistency_reviewer` will check three things:

1. Maya's design-doc identity supports this — she's described as someone who anchors on a single source she remembers. Yes.
2. The `cognitive_signal` text is a recognizable instance of Maya's voice, not a contradiction or a different pattern in disguise.
3. The corresponding `social_signal` follows the move/response pair rule — encoded as a move (Maya states the consensus prematurely) and a response (Jordan acquiesces) that planning_agent will encode in two adjacent `accomplishes` entries in `episode.yaml`'s turn outline.

If episode 2 instead tried to assign Maya a `cognitive_signal` for `confirmation_bias` ("Maya only listens to evidence she agrees with"), `story_consistency_reviewer` would flag the drift: the design doc never establishes Maya as a confirmation-bias person. The author would have to either pick a different carrier, change the cognitive_pattern target, or revise the design doc to add confirmation_bias to Maya's described tendencies (and add a setup beat in an earlier episode that earns it).

The point of walking through this is not the mechanics — those live in `framework/schemas/episode_plan.yaml` and `framework/pipeline/scripts/validate_story.py`. The point is that the design doc you write *up front* needs to commit Maya to specific tendencies clearly enough that filling in each episode's per-target signals is straightforward translation rather than improvisation. If episode 2 authoring forces you to invent a tendency Maya has not previously had, that is a signal the design doc was underspecified — not a signal that you should patch the design doc retroactively without re-checking earlier episodes.

## The information barrier, briefly

This document is design guidance, not pipeline architecture, but two facts about the information barrier shape what stories you can write. The full mechanism is in Part 5.5 of `framework/docs/story-pipeline-revision.md`; what you need to know as a story author is:

- **The dialog writer never sees the design doc, the per-episode draft, or `episode.yaml`.** It receives a barrier-safe projection (`episode_writer_input.yaml`) authored by `planning_agent`, which contains only persona voices, knowledge, weaknesses-as-prose, strengths-as-prose, and a turn outline whose `accomplishes` entries describe narrative beats without naming any framework category. No facet IDs, no lens names, no cognitive pattern labels, no social dynamic labels.
- **This means your `cognitive_signal` and `social_signal` prose must be stage directions, not analysis.** "Maya overstates how settled the question is from one remembered article" projects cleanly into the barrier-safe input as a behavioral cue the dialog writer can act on. "Maya exhibits false_certainty driven by overgeneralization" does not — it would either be stripped (losing the steering) or, worse, leak the framework category to the dialog writer (breaking the barrier). Two enforcement mechanisms catch the latter: a literal scan in `validate_schema.py` and a `projection_reviewer` agent that checks for paraphrased leakage. Neither alone is sufficient; both run on every projection.

The practical upshot for design: write per-target signals in the voice of *what the character does and how it sounds*, never in the voice of *what the framework categorizes it as*. The gap between those two voices is the information barrier, and the story system depends on operators staying on the right side of it.

## The two artifacts and how they relate

The **story design doc** (`framework/stories/{story_id}.md`) is the vision and the source of truth for character identity. It is written for humans, used for pitching the story to teachers, used for onboarding new operators, and used as the prose context every reviewer consults. It is committed to the repo as a first-class artifact.

The **per-episode drafts** (`framework/stories/{story_id}/episode_{NN}.md`) are the operator's authoring artifacts for what happens in each episode. They are also committed to the repo. The frontmatter is what `/create_episode` consumes; the prose body is what `story_consistency_reviewer` reads.

The two should not drift apart. `story_consistency_reviewer` reads both and verifies they agree — when the design doc says Sam grows out of conflict avoidance in episode 4, episodes 1–3 had better show pre-growth Sam and episode 4 had better show the inflection. If the prose chain is coherent, the design is well-formed. If the reviewer surfaces drift, you revise either the design doc or the offending episode draft (or both). Expect at least one revision pass on the design doc as a result of writing the per-episode drafts.
