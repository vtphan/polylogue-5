## Authorial notes

This is the season's stalemate ending and it has to do something specific that none of the previous episodes had to do: it has to refuse a tidy resolution without becoming a downer. The shared-room proposal is a *real* win — the maker space survives, the kids learned how to argue for it, the principal accepts the proposal — and it is also a real loss: the room is smaller in availability, the schedule is contested, some of what the kids loved is gone, and they end the episode worrying instead of celebrating. Both halves have to be visible to a 6th grader, and neither half can cancel the other.

The way I'm landing the stalemate is to put one weakness target (Dev's reflex slip) inside an episode that is otherwise built around four strengths. The strengths *outnumber* the weakness — the group has clearly learned things — and the weakness is *still there* — the group is clearly not transformed. The structural shape (4 strengths, 1 weakness, all rotated across different carriers) reads as "competence with residue," which is what stalemate is supposed to feel like.

The four strengths in this episode are deliberately distributed: Mira (relevance), Ren (sufficiency), Dev (inferential_validity), and Sam (perspective_engagement). Every lead character carries one strength, and the only character who carries a weakness is Dev. Theo carries no target this episode at all — he is in the room as a quiet presence and the closing beat ("it's a partial win and he is okay with that") is his coda for the season. He has done his work; episode 5 is not about him.

The principal's modification (a constraint about who closes up the room on shared days) is small but load-bearing: it tells the audience that even when the group has done everything right, the other side still has additions to make, and that is a feature of negotiating in good faith, not a flaw. The story is saying that good reasoning doesn't always produce the conclusion you started with — it produces a better conclusion than the one you would have gotten without it.

The closing beat is the one I am most worried about and the one that matters most. The group is not celebrating. Mira is already second-guessing the schedule. Sam is already worrying about kids she didn't ask. Ren is double-checking the source she already provided. Dev doesn't know an answer to a follow-up question. Theo says the only on-the-nose line in the season ("it's a partial win and I'm okay with that") and the audience has to land on the okay-with-that as honest, not as resignation. This is the season's argument compressed into one beat: good reasoning gets you a partial thing that nobody fully likes, and being okay with that is what learning to reason looks like at age twelve.

Sam's one line in this episode is the smallest she can do and still be visible. The growth from episode 4 is not erased by her brevity here — it is honored by it. A character whose major arc is "she can speak up when the stakes require it, not all the time" should not deliver a stirring monologue in the climax. She delivers one sentence. The principal underlines it. That is the right size.

I considered making the principal's modification *adversarial* — something the group has to push back on — and decided against it. The principal has been a model of good-faith engagement for three episodes; making her suddenly adversarial in episode 5 would betray her character. Her modification is a normal-sized addition any reasonable adult would make to a kids' proposal. The kids accept it without arguing. That is also part of what the season is teaching: not every disagreement is worth fighting, and recognizing the difference is a skill.

There is no moment of surprise in this episode. The moment of surprise lives in episode 3 (Theo) and the season has only one. Episode 5's job is the ending, and the ending earns its lack of tidy resolution by being smaller than the audience hoped and more honest than the audience expected.

## Why these targets

**relevance / Dev (weakness).** Dev has to carry the only weakness in this episode because his stable failure mode is the one most likely to *recur* under pressure — his reflex toward emotionally available framings. The point of the beat is not that Dev is failing, it is that the group has built a coordination mechanism around his reflex (the table tap with Mira) and the mechanism works. The relevance lens is scope, not evidence, because in this episode the question is not "what evidence are we citing" but "whose concerns is the proposal addressing" — that is a scope question even when the carrier is Dev. The interaction_note flags that the reflex hasn't gone away; that's the stalemate marker.

**relevance (strength) / Mira.** Mira's stable strength per the design doc is "she keeps the group focused on what would persuade the principal." Episode 5 is the episode where that strength is finally usable, because the group has learned what *would* persuade the principal. Mira's organizing instinct, which produced the premature-consensus failures across episodes 1–4, now produces the table tap. Same instinct, different application — the season is not about Mira changing, it is about the group learning to use Mira's instinct correctly. The note calls this out explicitly so the reviewer doesn't read it as a transformation.

**sufficiency (strength) / Ren.** Ren's growth arc landing has to be a sufficiency strength because her weakness was source_credibility and her growth has been from "I bring whatever I find" to "I check what I bring." The maintenance-log photo is the beat that proves the growth — she didn't just check the source, she preserved the citation in a form she could produce on demand. The note flags that her growth shows up as *preparation*, which is the specific way her arc differs from Sam's: Ren's growth is a habit, Sam's growth is a single hard intervention. Both are real and both are appropriately scaled to the characters.

**inferential_validity (strength) / Dev.** Dev's stable strength per the design doc is "he can deliver a presentation when the moment requires it," which is hard to express as a facet strength on its own. The way to land it is through inferential_validity: Dev's episode 2 weakness was that his arguments didn't follow from his premises; episode 5's strength is that the *proposal* he delivers does. The argument follows now because the group has done the work between episodes 4 and 5. Dev's job is to deliver the argument cleanly, and he does. The strength is real because the proposal earns its conclusion, not because Dev has become rigorous.

**perspective_engagement (strength) / Sam.** Sam's growth arc has to extend into episode 5 in some form, and the right form is small. One sentence in the principal's office, on the question of the kids on the Tuesday schedule who want the room open after school. The principal underlining the line is the audience's cue that Sam's contribution mattered; Sam's worrying on the walk back is the audience's cue that the growth is costly. The note specifies the exact line so the reviewer can check the eventual transcript against it.
