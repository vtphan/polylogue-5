Polylogue — A Critical Thinking Project for Middle School

What it is. Polylogue helps 6th–7th graders learn to evaluate the quality of group reasoning. Students read short, AI-generated peer discussions (10–14 turns, about 3 minutes of reading) on age-appropriate topics, then work passage-by-passage to articulate what's strong, what's weak, and why people reasoned the way they did.

The big idea. Critical thinking isn't taught as a list of fallacies to memorize. It's taught as perspectival learning: students commit to their own reading first, encounter peers who see it differently, then meet an AI "perspective" (not an answer key), and finally a teacher synthesis. Disagreement is the lesson.

WHAT STUDENTS LEARN TO SEE

Students learn to look at any discussion through three lenses:
- The Logic lens asks whether the conclusion actually follows.
- The Evidence lens asks whether the claim is backed up by something trustworthy.
- The Scope lens asks whether the thinkers looked broadly enough — at other views, consequences, and exceptions.

THE THINKING FLAWS STUDENTS LEARN TO RECOGNIZE

Each lens points to specific ways reasoning can break down. These are the flaws students learn to spot:

Under the Evidence lens:
- Shaky sources — leaning on sources that aren't trustworthy.
- Narrow sources — hearing from only one side or one kind of voice.
- Off-target evidence — citing facts that don't actually support the claim.
- Thin evidence — too little to justify the conclusion being drawn.

Under the Logic lens:
- Leaps in reasoning — conclusions that don't follow from what was said.
- Contradictions — claims that quietly clash with each other.
- Missing steps — important parts of the argument left unsaid.

Under the Scope lens:
- Ignoring other viewpoints — never engaging with people who would disagree.
- Overlooking consequences — not thinking through what could happen next.
- Missing limits — treating an idea as if it works in every situation.

Students don't memorize these names. They notice the problem in the discussion, then learn to put it into their own words.

WHY REASONING GOES WRONG: TWO EXPLANATORY FORCES

The approach also gives students a way to ask why people may reason poorly. Two forces are usually at work — habits inside our own heads, and pressures from the group around us.

Cognitive patterns — habits inside one person's head:
- Confirmation bias - only noticing what supports what you already believe. 
- Tunnel vision - fixating on one angle and missing the bigger picture. 
- Overgeneralization - drawing sweeping conclusions from too little evidence. 
- False cause - assuming "connected" means "caused by." 
- Uncritical acceptance - believing a claim without checking. 
- Black-and-white thinking - seeing two options where there are many. 
- Egocentric thinking - only considering perspectives like your own. 
- False certainty - sounding sure without grounds to be.

Social dynamics — pressures that come from the group:
- Group pressure - converging on a view because everyone else seems to. 
- Conflict avoidance - backing down from a point to keep things comfortable. 
- Authority deference - letting the loudest or most confident voice decide.

A central insight students take away: weak reasoning is usually both at once — a cognitive habit amplified by a social one.

HOW A CLASS SESSION WORKS (about 50 minutes)

For each of 3–4 highlighted passages, every student moves through the same arc. First, students diagnose privately: they pick a lens, rate the passage strong or weak, and write why, with no peer visibility yet so no one anchors on anyone else. Next, the table discusses: diagnoses become visible, the group debates, and they may spend a "lifeline" for a graduated hint. Then they meet the AI perspective — a short, fallible reading from the AI, framed as one voice among several rather than the verdict. The group submits an assessment, which is always revisable. Finally, the teacher leads a whole-class debrief that synthesizes across groups and closes the loop. The teacher, not the AI, holds final authority.

WHAT THE TEACHER RECEIVES

For every scenario, the pipeline produces a ready-to-run session packet: the discussion, the passages worth examining, common student misreadings, graduated hints, a facilitation guide, and discussion prompts for the debrief. Teachers run the session — they don't have to author it.

WHY IT'S DIFFERENT

Polylogue uses discussions rather than worksheets, so students evaluate real-feeling peer talk instead of isolated claim-and-evidence exercises. Students must articulate before judging — saying what they see before being told what to look for. Once students can name forces like group pressure or confirmation bias, they start spotting them outside class — in news, in lunchroom arguments, in themselves. And there is no single right answer: the goal is sharper perception and richer explanation, not converging on the teacher's reading.
