# Strangers in the Old Forest

## Premise

Four 6th graders in Memphis see a red-eyed white squirrel in Overton Park one Saturday afternoon, and one of them — Jules — can't let it go. Her older brother Marcus, an astrobiology student at Rhodes, knows a physics Ph.D. candidate at the University of Memphis named Anya whose side research is on *cryptobiotic signatures in urban wildlife populations* — the idea that unusual color morphs in isolated urban animals could be candidate biosignatures of environmental anomaly. Jules calls Anya that night. By Monday the four friends are chasing an alien hypothesis they did not quite invent and an anxiety they cannot quite name: something might be happening in their park, and the adults around them don't seem to be noticing. Over ten episodes the group investigates — they count squirrels, they remember what their 6th-grade science teacher Ms. Reyes taught them about genes and alleles, they write inferential chains in a notebook, they plot sightings on a map, they discover they've been walking only the south side of the park, they walk the north, they call Anya back with sharper questions, and they arrive at a real answer: a long-ago mutation in a grey squirrel ancestor, preserved by founder effect in a habitat patch that highways cut off from the rest of Memphis, passed silently through generations of heterozygous carriers, finally visible as red-eyed homozygotes in numbers large enough for 6th graders to notice.

The story is about what happens when a credentialed adult leads you toward a frightening answer and you have to reason your way back to a boring one. It is also about what happens when you are scared and no one but your three best friends is taking the question seriously. The critical thinking the kids learn is not a performance and not a trick — it is how they figure out whether to be scared. By episode 10 they have used logic to reconstruct a chain, evidence to audit a source, and scope to notice what they had not been looking at, and they have arrived at the mundane answer that was always going to be the right one. The relief is real. So is the answer.

## Setting

Overton Park in Memphis, Tennessee — the Old Forest, the Greensward, Rhodes College a few blocks north, the zoo adjacent. Real place, real squirrels, real 131 acres of urban forest that 6th graders actually bike to after school. The park is specific in its details (the shelter by the playground, the path that loops the forest, the fence along the zoo's back, the road that cuts the north side from the south) because the mystery depends on place: which parts of the park the kids walk, and which parts they don't, is the whole scope question, and the *isolation* of the park from the rest of Memphis's tree cover is the whole genetics answer.

The four kids go to the same middle school in Midtown Memphis and come from four different parts of the city — Jules in Cooper-Young, Maya in South Memphis, Cam in Binghampton, Priya in East Memphis. Home locations matter to the story because what each kid knows and does not know is shaped by which Memphis they come from. The action happens in the park and at an outdoor picnic table where they spread out their notebooks. No scene happens inside anyone's home. No adult ever speaks on-screen in any transcript. Jules's mother, her brother Marcus, Priya's parents, Cam's mother, and Maya's father are referenced in the kids' dialogue; Anya the grad student is on the other end of three phone calls that different kids take at different times; Ms. Reyes the 6th-grade science teacher is quoted from memory. None of them has a speaking turn. The kids' world is a kid world.

### Friendship

The four have been the four of them since 4th grade, two years ago, when they were all in Ms. Reyes's class and she ran a year-long nature-journal project in Overton Park. Each kid picked a species and tracked it across the school year. They paired up for field visits. By the end of fifth grade the four had cohered into a group that outlasted the project, and they have been meeting at the park every Wednesday after school and most weekends since. They do not have a name for themselves; they just say "the four of us." The park is not just where the mystery happens — it is where their friendship happens. Overton is their place.

This matters in three concrete ways that the dialog writer should trust as background texture without over-narrating. First, Ms. Reyes has been their teacher for two years running; her lens-shaped questions ("*what would have to be true for that answer to be the right one?*" / "*where did that come from, and who said it first?*" / "*who isn't in the room when you made that conclusion?*") are not fresh classroom material but a shared idiom the four of them carry. Any of them can quote her, and sometimes they do, and it sounds like muscle memory rather than citation. Second, when Jules is scared in episode 2 about what the red-eyed squirrel means, her three oldest friends are the only people in her life who treat her fear as a real question; their parents are distracted, the internet is dismissive, and even Marcus is more interested in the physics than the worry. That isolation is part of why the investigation continues — and part of why pushing back on Jules is not an abstract act of critique but a small risk to a two-year friendship. Third, something strange happening in Overton Park is not a mystery in some park; it is a mystery in the place that is them. None of this needs to be said in transcripts. It just shapes how they talk.

## Cast

There are four recurring characters. Each episode features two, three, or all four of them; the final episode features all four in the same scene. Anya is heard about but never heard, quoted but never present — the dialog writer encounters her only as reported speech inside the kids' turns. Ms. Reyes is the same.

### Jules

Jules is the engine. She is the reason the investigation exists, the reason it does not stop, and the reason the other three are in the park on a Wednesday afternoon instead of at home. On the Saturday the four of them saw the red-eyed white squirrel, it was Jules who froze and said *its eyes*, and Jules who couldn't stop thinking about it, and Jules who called her brother Marcus that night, and Marcus who put her in touch with Anya. Her mother is a freelance science writer who makes a living writing curious-weird-science pieces for kids' magazines — the kind of mom who shows you a video of tardigrades at breakfast and reads papers about extremophiles out loud during long car rides. Marcus has walked her through the Old Forest explaining what SETI was looking for. Jules has grown up on a worldview where anomalies matter and the boring explanation is sometimes the lazy one. That is a *real epistemology* and the story treats it that way.

Her weaknesses are `false_cause` and `black_and_white_thinking`. When Jules sees something weird her mind pattern-matches on surface similarity: red eyes look wrong, wrong-looking is a sign something changed them, something must have caused the change, the cause is probably unknown because science is full of unknowns — and the chain feels tight to her because her mother's articles are full of exactly this kind of chain. What her mother's articles also do — and what Jules has not quite absorbed — is the work of asking what would have to be true for each link to hold. Jules has inherited the conclusions and the enthusiasm without the process. Her `black_and_white_thinking` is the adjacent move: in her head the question is *aliens or nothing*, where *nothing* means boring, and if she gives up on aliens she has nothing interesting left. That framing is what makes the mundane answer feel like a defeat before she has heard it properly.

Jules also carries `authority_deference`, and in this story it is load-bearing. The authority is Anya. Over three phone calls — episode 2, episode 8, episode 10 — Jules takes the first and last; Cam takes the middle one. Jules's first call happens before the story starts and is reported in episode 2; her retelling of what Anya said emphasizes the parts that fit her already-forming hypothesis and softens the caveats. That retelling is not dishonest. It is what `authority_deference` on top of `false_cause` and `black_and_white_thinking` produces in a specific head at a specific moment — she hears Anya through her own filter, and what she passes to the group is Anya-as-Jules-heard-her. The group accepts Jules's retelling without asking who Anya is.

**Jules does not have a growth arc.** She stays Jules across the season. What changes around her is that the other three do the reasoning work, and by episode 10 the reasoning has become unignorable — the data fits genetics, not aliens, and Jules is not the kind of person who fakes her way past evidence her friends have walked her through. She accepts the answer. She is not humiliated, she is not corrected, she is not "wrong" in the way stories sometimes punish characters for being wrong; she is a smart curious kid whose best friends have shown her what the reasoning looks like when it is finished. Her final move in the final episode is to call Anya back and report what the group figured out, and to notice without commentary that Anya sounds defensive on the other end of the line. That noticing is as close as Jules gets to growing in this story, and it is enough.

### Maya

Maya is the Logic grower. Her dad is a pastor at a small neighborhood church in South Memphis and her mom works in logistics operations at FedEx, and Maya has been around arguments her whole life — sermons that had to land, family dinners where her mom walked through why a delivery route was wrong. She can *feel* when an argument works. What she has never been asked to do is formalize why. Her instinct is strong enough that nobody pushes her to explain, and so she has never built the muscle. She says things like *that doesn't sound right* and *that feels off* and is usually correct, which is the problem.

Maya's weakness lives at the facet level: she reasons with `inferential_validity` that is real but informal, she closes chains before they are finished (`reasoning_completeness`), and across episodes her claims drift in ways she does not notice until someone reads them back to her (`internal_consistency`). She does not exhibit a named cognitive pattern — her weakness is not a fallacy shape, it is a *habit* of stopping when the answer feels right. That is a subtler thing and the story trusts it to be visible because Maya's inflection is physical: she writes on-page, and the writing itself is the turn.

Maya's growth runs across three episodes. In episode 4, trying to apply what the group remembers of Ms. Reyes's Mendelian genetics unit, she cannot reconstruct the chain from *recessive allele* to *observable red-eyed phenotype* even though she can feel it should work; the Logic weakness is fully planted. In episode 5 she writes the chain out step by step in the notebook for the first time in her life — clunky and slow — and in the middle of writing she and Cam together notice that something she said in episode 2 or 3 does not fit what she is writing now. Cam reads the earlier sentence back to her; she pauses, revises, keeps going. That is the inflection. In episode 9 she constructs a second chain — on mutation, founder effect, heterozygous carriers, and habitat isolation — faster and cleaner than the first, and nobody has to push her to write it down. She now writes by default. By episode 10 she is the kid who formalizes things without being asked, because she has learned that her father's rule — *show your work in a sermon or nobody trusts you* — applies to her own thinking as well as to preaching.

Maya also carries the story's `group_pressure` social-dynamic texture in its friend-protective form. In episodes 2 and 3, when Jules is visibly scared, Maya can feel something is off in Jules's retelling of Anya but softens how hard she pushes. That softening is not cowardice and not agreement; it is a 6th-grader navigating *how much to press on a friend who is frightened*. The group pressure is Jules's fear, not Jules's social dominance. Part of what Maya learns across the season — alongside her Logic arc — is that caring about a friend sometimes means pushing back on them more, not less.

Maya's carried facets are `inferential_validity`, `reasoning_completeness`, and `internal_consistency`, moving from weaknesses in episodes 4 and the first half of 5 to growing strengths across episodes 5, 9, and 10. Lens: Logic.

### Cam

Cam is the Evidence grower. He lives in Binghampton. His mom is an ER nurse at Methodist; his dad works construction. Cam had a phone earlier than the other three — partly because his parents' schedules meant he was unsupervised more in the evenings — and he spends more time than anyone in the group on social media. His information environment is a scroll: a TikTok, a news clip, a meme, a friend's cousin, all arriving through the same feed with the same visual weight. He treats sources as interchangeable because *his whole informational life* has treated them as interchangeable. This is not a character flaw and the story refuses to frame it as one. It is a faithful description of how a 6th grader on a phone in 2026 encounters information.

His primary weakness is `uncritical_acceptance`. In episode 1 he cites a TikTok about the park as if it were equivalent to a news article; in episode 2 he accepts Jules's retelling of Anya without asking who Anya actually is. Nobody pushes back at first because his tone is confident and the claims are interesting. His growth is not *stop using the phone* — the story would be dishonest to expect that — but learning that *the phone is a pile of sources, not a source*, and learning that the same provenance question applies to credentialed adults as to creators online. Asking where a claim came from is a move you can make without being the kid who slows everything down.

Cam's secondary weakness is `overgeneralization`, surfacing cleanly in episode 3 when he posts about the red-eyed squirrels online and the post goes mildly viral within his small follower count. From a handful of ambiguous replies — some mocking, some serious, one claiming a similar sighting in a different park across town — Cam concludes the sightings are *everywhere now*. That move produces a weak `sufficiency` value in the same episode: a few online replies, from strangers, with unverified claims, is not enough to support the geography he reads into them. Coordinate with the overgeneralization in that same episode is `confirmation_bias`, planted on Cam specifically because he posted the hypothesis and has a small stake in being right. His reading of the replies runs *inside* his hypothesis rather than on it: supportive comments get quoted twice and the Frayser cross-town sighting gets foregrounded, while the mocking replies get waved away as *just being mean* and the curious-skeptical replies get skipped entirely. The confirmation bias also implicates `source_diversity` — he is treating downstream responses to his own post framing as independent sources. These four moves (overgeneralization, confirmation_bias, weak sufficiency, weak source_diversity) land in one scroll in one scene, and they are secondary surfaces that show up in episode 3 and fade — not a separate arc, just the specific shape beginner mistakes take when social media is the information environment. Cam's confirmation_bias is episode-scoped; Anya's, which is the season-level case the kids eventually reason their way out of, is not the same size of claim and should not be read as competing with it.

Cam's growth runs across two beats. In episode 2 — small, begrudging — he comes back having actually *looked up* the creator of one of his TikTok sources after his mom pushed him to, and reports what he found with visible annoyance at being nudged. That beat is the seed. In episode 8 — the real beat — he calls Anya himself, unprompted by any adult, and asks her plainly what her dissertation is on, who her advisor is, and whether her biosignature work is a main line or a side project. What he finds out — her dissertation is on planetary atmospheres, her advisor is a planetary physicist, the biosignature stuff is conference-side-work — he reports back to the group, and it recasts everything the group has accepted from Jules's ep-2 retelling. In the same episode Cam does the move the story has been waiting for: he audits his own ep-3 post. The provenance question applies to his account too. That self-audit is the confirmation that his growth is real and not externally driven.

Cam also carries the `group_pressure` texture, in its *public-commitment* form. His ep-3 post created a tiny public constituency for the alien hypothesis — his followers, some of whom mocked the kids, some of whom doubled down in agreement. Updating the investigation's conclusion is no longer a purely private move for him; there is a cost to posting the correction. That cost is not enormous. It is real enough to be realistic.

Cam's carried facets are `source_credibility`, `source_diversity`, and `sufficiency` (the last two specifically in episode 3, alongside his `overgeneralization` + `confirmation_bias` moves there), appearing as weaknesses in episodes 1 through 3 and as growing strengths in episodes 8 and 10. His ep-8 growth slightly overcorrects — he wants the group to audit everything and slows them down — because new skills are clumsy. Lens: Evidence.

### Priya

Priya is the Scope grower, and in this story her weakness is the most *physical* of the cast — it lives in where she has walked and where she has not. She lives in East Memphis, near Audubon Park. Both of her parents are University of Memphis faculty — her mom in biology (a lever for the story: she is how the group can check on some of what Ms. Reyes taught), her dad in economics. At the start of the season Priya is already good at Logic and Evidence. She brings real sources. Her arguments hold up. She is, in the group's mental picture, the one who knows things.

Priya's weakness lives at the facet level as a `condition_sensitivity` deficit: she draws conclusions without tracking which conditions her data was collected under. In episode 4, when the group is applying what they remember of Ms. Reyes's genetics unit, Priya says something like *we've only ever seen red-eyed squirrels in the Old Forest, so that's where they live* — a sentence that is true about the observation and untrue about the conclusion, because the Old Forest is where they have walked. The sentence lands without anyone pushing back because Priya usually knows what she is talking about. That is her visible weak move, and it is the anchor for her arc.

Priya's growth is then set up by her *absence* in episode 6, which is deliberate. The group is plotting sightings on a map of the park, and the scope problem — they have been walking only the south side, they have been mapping only their walk — is visible to the reader, but none of the three present kids quite names it. The one person in the group who would have caught it, given a nudge, is Priya; she is not there, and the reason she is not there is part of what she has to learn. In episode 7 the four of them walk the north side of Overton Park together, and it is Priya who names the sampling bias out loud: *we were drawing a map of the part of the park we walk.* That is her inflection. It is as much about the Memphis she has not walked as about the park she has not walked. By the second half of the season Priya carries `perspective_engagement` as a growing strength — the habit of asking whose walk is missing, whose part of town is missing, whose part of the data is missing.

Priya's carried facets are `condition_sensitivity` and `perspective_engagement`. She has no named cognitive pattern; her weakness is not a fallacy shape but a habit of not tracking context — the scope-lens analogue of Maya's logic-lens habit of not finishing chains. The story does not need to label it to make it visible. It needs to put her in front of the south-side map without her, and then to walk the north side with her.

**Scaffolding note for downstream design.** Priya's inflection is the most physical in the cast and the easiest to miss because her weakness is structural rather than behavioral. `scaffolding_id` should treat episodes 4 and 6 as calibration targets: episode 4 because her weak move is a single sentence that reads as an observation if read quickly, and episode 6 because her absence is doing structural work a hint system has to point at without giving away the answer. Hints for both episodes need to direct attention toward *whose walk is missing* without naming `condition_sensitivity` outright.

### Anya (off-screen)

Anya is a Ph.D. candidate in the physics department at the University of Memphis. Her dissertation is on planetary atmospheres and her advisor is a planetary physicist. Her *side research* — the work she presents at small regional astrobiology colloquia, the work Marcus knows her from — is on what she calls *cryptobiotic signatures in urban wildlife populations*: the hypothesis that unusual color morphs in isolated urban animal populations could be candidate biosignatures of environmental anomaly, panspermia seeding, or other non-terrestrial influence. She is credentialed, genuinely smart, speaks beautifully about Drake equations and the Fermi paradox, and is wrong about the squirrels.

Her flaw is `confirmation_bias` in textbook form. Three years into a side project whose premise is *rarity-as-signature*, Anya's attention has trained itself to notice anomalies and to underweight the mundane baseline — a 6th-grade science teacher would tell you that rare things are still usually explained by rare-but-ordinary causes, but Anya's framework privileges the interesting over the probable. She is not lying and she is not grandstanding. She genuinely believes her framework and she genuinely thinks the red-eyed Overton squirrels are a candidate case worth checking. Nothing Anya says is *false* in a sentence-by-sentence sense; what is wrong is the weight she puts on the framing relative to the unglamorous alternative.

Anya never appears on-screen. The dialog writer encounters her only as **reported speech inside the kids' turns**, in three phone calls:

- **Episode 2 (retold, not present).** Jules took the first call over the weekend; episode 2 is her retelling. What Jules reports is Anya-as-Jules-heard-her, filtered through Jules's own `false_cause` and `black_and_white_thinking`. The group accepts the retelling.
- **Episode 8 (retold, not present).** Cam calls Anya himself and asks plainly what her dissertation is on and whether biosignature work is a main line or a side project. What he reports back differs in emphasis from what Jules reported — not because Anya said different things, but because two different listeners asked different questions and carried home different summaries. This is the structural move that lets 6th graders *see* confirmation bias in the delta between Jules's summary and Cam's summary without needing to hear Anya directly.
- **Episode 10 (retold, not present).** Jules calls Anya back with the group's conclusion. Anya is defensive on the line; she reframes her earlier claims to protect them from the evidence the group presents. Jules notices this and does not press. That noticing is the story's final word on what it means to have outgrown an authority.

Anya is not a villain. She is a smart person with a specific kind of blind spot, and the point is that smart people have blind spots and the reasoning tools the kids are learning are exactly how you check an expert's claims against the world. A 6th grader should walk out of this story understanding that *auditing a credentialed source is not rude*, and that the reasoning lenses apply to Ph.D. candidates the same way they apply to TikToks.

## Arc summary

The ten episodes trace a single investigation from a red-eyed sighting to an honest genetic answer, with the alien hypothesis peaking around episode 2 and losing one foothold at a time until episode 9, when the mundane answer is fully assembled and the anxiety loses its last purchase.

**Episode 1 — The play date.** The four of them at the picnic table after school on the Monday following the Saturday sighting. They retell what happened — who saw it first, what it looked like, *its eyes*, what they did after. Weak moves plant cleanly: Jules leaps from red eyes to *something changed it* (`false_cause` chain on a base of one observation, which makes this the season's purest `sufficiency` weakness); Cam cites a TikTok he saw that weekend about Overton as if the TikTok is evidence; Priya generalizes from the parks she knows (*squirrels don't have red eyes anywhere I've been*), a soft version of her condition-sensitivity weakness that foreshadows episode 4; and Maya writes the question in her notebook and the other three do not engage with what she has written. *Shape: early-strength-collapse.* Evidence-primary.

**Episode 2 — The phone call.** Jules reports what Anya told her on Saturday night. Her retelling emphasizes the biosignature framing and softens the caveats; Anya's actual identity (physics, not biology) goes unremarked because Jules did not ask. The group absorbs the alien hypothesis as if it came from a credentialed source. Cam accepts the retelling without asking who Anya is — his `uncritical_acceptance` applied to a *credentialed* voice, which is the move he has not yet learned to make. Maya feels something is off in Jules's retelling and softens her pushback because Jules is visibly scared — the season's first `group_pressure` beat in its friend-protective form. Jules voices her fear out loud: *what if it's happening and no one notices.* *Shape: unresolved disagreement.* Evidence-primary.

**Episode 3 — The post.** Cam posts about the red-eyed squirrels on his account, partly because Jules seems so certain and partly because it is the kind of thing his feed rewards. Responses come in: mocking, curious, one person claiming a sighting in a park across town. From the handful Cam concludes the sightings are *everywhere now* (`overgeneralization` + `confirmation_bias` + weak `sufficiency` and `source_diversity` — he reads his own-post replies as independent sources and quotes the supportive ones while waving away the skeptical). Adults in the kids' lives — parents, older siblings overheard — dismiss the whole thing; the *nobody-is-listening* feeling lands hard. Maya surfaces Ms. Reyes — *remember what she said about recessive traits, like the thing we were supposed to apply to real life.* The group tentatively agrees to try to apply it tomorrow. *Shape: stalemate.* Evidence-primary. (Alien-anxiety peak.)

**Episode 4 — What Ms. Reyes said.** Tuesday afternoon at the picnic table. The four of them try to apply what they remember of the Mendelian genetics unit: genotype and phenotype, dominant and recessive, mutation as a rare heritable change, the *two-copies* Punnett logic. Maya tries to connect it to the red-eyed squirrel and cannot reconstruct the chain cleanly — her Logic weakness is fully planted. Priya makes her condition-sensitivity weak move (*we've only ever seen them in the Old Forest, so that's where they live*), which the group does not push back on because Priya is usually right. Cam, prompted by his mom's provenance nudge from the night before, notes without conviction that he looked up one of his TikTok sources and it was a kid running a comedy account — a small seed. Somebody (likely Maya) catches themselves mid-sentence deferring to Anya's framing and stops — a small self-correction. *Shape: small self-correction.* Logic-primary.

**Episode 5 — The notebook.** Maya writes the inferential chain out step by step for the first time in her life — slow, clunky, with false starts she crosses out. In the middle of writing, Cam — reading over her shoulder — notices that a sentence Maya wrote in the notebook on Monday does not fit what she is writing now. He reads the earlier sentence back to her. Maya pauses, revises, keeps going. She catches her own `internal_consistency` on-page, and Cam has peer-audited another kid's earlier claim for the first time — a small expansion of his Evidence arc into auditing statements, not just online sources. The group sees Maya do something she visibly could not do in episode 4: finish a chain. *Shape: strength-prevails.* Logic-primary.

**Episode 6 — The map.** Priya is absent — a family commitment her mom mentioned. The three remaining kids plot every sighting they've collected onto a map of the park. The clustering is clean: all on the south-side paths that lead from the picnic table through the Old Forest's main loop. Maya's ep-5 chain fits the clustering and the group is tempted to call the mystery solved; Maya herself is tempted — the chain is hers, the data fits, the notebook can close. Maya resists by rereading the original question she wrote in episode 1 and noticing that the question was broader than *where do red-eyed squirrels live in the part of the park we walk*. The scope problem is on the page and nobody quite names it. The notebook stays open. *Shape: stalemate.* Scope-primary.

**Episode 7 — The north side.** All four of them. They walk a path through Overton that none of them has walked before — the north side, across the road from the playground. Priya is the one who names the sampling bias out loud: *we were drawing a map of the part of the park we walk.* That is her inflection. The group agrees to count what they see on the north-side walk. What they see does not fit any single hypothesis cleanly yet — but what they also see is the *edge* of the park, where the forest ends at a road and does not continue on the other side. Priya carries this into scope as more than a moment: she notices, without prompting, that the park has been cut off from the rest of Memphis's tree cover by the highways and development they bike across every day. That sentence is the seed for episode 9. *Shape: distributed self-correction.* Scope-primary.

**Episode 8 — The second call.** Cam calls Anya himself. He asks plainly: *what's your dissertation actually on? who's your advisor? is the biosignature thing your main research or a side thing?* Anya answers (planetary atmospheres; a planetary physicist; side project — she is honest about this when asked directly). Cam reports back. Jules has to face that the authority she carried home from Saturday night was a physicist talking about biology as a hobby. In the same conversation Cam audits his own ep-3 post — the provenance question turned on himself — and proposes to take it down or add a correction later. Maya softens her critique of Jules's initial framing more than the evidence warrants, because Jules is visibly rattled; the `group_pressure` texture returns, and this time Priya names it gently (*we can be nice to Jules and still say what we found*). *Shape: distributed self-correction.* Evidence-primary.

**Episode 9 — The lineage.** Maya builds the second chain. Qualitatively: a rare mutation in the gene that makes melanin — the same kind of change Ms. Reyes said happens occasionally — arose long ago in a grey squirrel ancestor, on one locus, recessive. The park is a habitat island — the highways cut the Old Forest off from the rest of Memphis's tree cover years ago. A small founder population carried the allele; grey squirrels look grey whether they carry one copy of the white allele or none; the allele has passed silently through generations of heterozygous carriers. Isolation has kept it from being diluted out. When two carriers mate, one in four of their offspring is homozygous — a red-eyed white squirrel, because albinism affects eye pigmentation the same way it affects fur. The pattern of sightings, the clustering, the presence of red-eyed squirrels only in Overton and not in other Memphis parks, the quiet accumulation over time — everything the four of them have seen fits. Jules watches this assembly happen without her and is quiet. Cam points out one more time that nothing in Maya's chain requires anything non-terrestrial. The alien-anxiety loses its last foothold. *Shape: strength-prevails.* Logic-primary.

**Episode 10 — The call back.** All four of them in the park on Thursday afternoon. They construct, together, the falsifiable reformulation: *aliens would predict red-eyed squirrels appearing in other parks, spreading outward, increasing over time; genetics predicts stable clustering in this park specifically, tied to its isolation and the carrier frequency the founder population happened to have. The data fits genetics.* The reformulation is a collective move, not Jules's solo growth; Jules accepts the answer because her three best friends have walked her through the reasoning, not because she has become a different kind of thinker. Maya makes the third phone call to Anya on speakerphone; Anya reframes her earlier claims defensively, moving the goalposts in small ways. Jules notices without pressing. Cam posts a correction to his ep-3 post and the post gets fewer replies than the original. Maya reads the original question from episode 1 back to the group. They close the notebook. The fear has dissolved into something closer to relief, not triumph. *Shape: strength-prevails at the season level.*

### Lens primacy

Evidence primary: episodes 1, 2, 3, 8. Logic primary: episodes 4, 5, 9, 10. Scope primary: episodes 6, 7. No lens carries more than half the episodes; each carries at least two. Logic's weight in the back half is load-bearing — Maya's growth is the most demanding in the cast and needs three beats (5, 9, 10) to earn.

### Mixed-valence shape rotation

All five shapes appear; no shape carries more than three episodes. Early-strength-collapse: 1. Unresolved disagreement: 2. Stalemate: 3, 6. Small self-correction: 4. Distributed self-correction: 7, 8. Strength-prevails: 5, 9, 10.

## Stakes

There is nothing concrete to lose in this story — the park is not closing, the squirrels are not endangered, no adult is threatening anything the four of them care about. What is at stake is emotional and epistemic, and for 11-year-olds it is more than enough.

What is at stake, first, is whether Jules is safe. The alien hypothesis, at its root, is a frightening hypothesis — *something is here and no one is noticing* — and Jules carries that fear under the curiosity across the first third of the season. The adults around her do not notice the fear because the adults around her do not take the question seriously enough to see it as a question someone could be afraid of the answer to. Her three best friends take the question seriously, and that taking-seriously is what lets the fear surface at all. When the genetic answer assembles in episode 9 and the alien hypothesis loses its last foothold, what dissolves is not Jules's curiosity — she is still the kid who wants the weird to be real — but the fear underneath it. The fear dissolving is the emotional payoff of the season.

What is at stake, second, is the friendship. Four kids who have been the four of them since 4th grade, who met in Ms. Reyes's class and cohered around a nature-journal project in this park, are the only people who take Jules's fear seriously. That means pushing back on Jules is not an abstract critique — it is a small risk to the thing that matters most to them outside their families. The `group_pressure` texture that runs through Maya and Cam is the story's honest account of what it costs to say *I don't think your reasoning closes* to a friend who is scared. Part of what the season teaches, alongside logic and evidence and scope, is that real caring sometimes means pushing back more, not less, and that the friendship is strong enough to hold the pushback.

What is at stake, third, is whether the four of them can do something adults did not take seriously enough to do themselves. The adults in their lives — parents, older siblings, the dismissive internet — did not listen. Ms. Reyes listens but is not there. Anya listened and pointed the wrong way. What the four of them have, they have because they built it: the notebook, the map, the north-side walk, the second phone call, the chain. The answer they arrive at is correct. They arrive at it on a thinner thread than a real biologist would tolerate, and the story's final judgment is on the quality of their reasoning path, not on whether they were right.

## Pedagogical commitments

This story is built around six things students should walk away noticing.

**One: thin reasoning is not wrong reasoning — it is reasoning that hasn't finished yet.** Every character in the cast starts the season with a genuinely weak move, and the story refuses to treat those weak moves as failures of character. Jules's alien hypothesis is earned from her family and her fear; Maya's feel-first reasoning is earned from a household full of arguments that worked by feel; Cam's interchangeable sources are earned from the information environment he actually lives in; Priya's local reasoning is earned from a life spent in one part of the city. The right response to any of these is not *you're wrong* but *show me more*, and the growth beats are structured as *doing more work*, not as *admitting fault*.

**Two: growth is earned on-screen, not narrated.** A character saying *I learned my lesson* is cheap. A character in episode 5 doing a thing they visibly could not do in episode 4, without anyone prompting them, is the real thing. Each of the three lens-growers (Maya, Cam, Priya) has a specific on-screen move that marks their inflection — Maya writing the chain, Cam calling Anya unprompted, Priya walking the north side of the park — and each move is set up by an earlier episode where the same character failed to make the move. Students watching the season should be able to point at both ends of each arc.

**Three: the visible layer is the tip of a hidden layer.** Genotype and phenotype are the story's conceptual payoff, and they rhyme with the Polylogue framework's own architecture — visible moves in a conversation are the surface expression of hidden reasoning, exactly as red-eyed white squirrels in Overton Park are the visible expression of a recessive allele that has been carried silently by every grey squirrel in the founder population for generations. A reader who notices this resonance has understood something about reasoning, not just something about squirrels.

**Four: experts can be wrong, and the lenses work on them too.** Anya is credentialed, smart, and genuinely mistaken. The kids reason their way out of the position she led them into, and by episode 10 Jules has learned — without it being said — that *Anya said so* is not a closing move. Students should walk away understanding that auditing a credentialed source is not rude, and that the reasoning tools they are building apply to Ph.D. candidates the same way they apply to TikToks. This is the story's specific case against authority deference: not that authorities are untrustworthy in general, but that any individual authority's claims have to be checked the way any individual claim does.

**Five: good reasoning produces honest answers, not confident ones — and it sometimes tells you to stop being scared.** The season's final episode does not end with a triumphant announcement. It ends with the four of them assembling a falsifiable reformulation together, Maya calling Anya back and noticing defensiveness without pressing on it, Cam posting a quiet correction, and the fear that has shadowed Jules since Saturday night dissolving into something closer to relief. Nobody claims certainty. The answer is right and the thread is thinner than anyone would like, and saying so is the most grown-up move in the story.

**Six: critical thinking is how you figure out whether to be scared.** This is the story's most 6th-grade commitment. The alien hypothesis was frightening. It made Jules feel like something real was happening and no adult was noticing. The way out of that fear was not an adult saying *don't worry*; it was the four of them doing the reasoning work themselves until the genetic answer had assembled and there was nothing left for the fear to live in. The lenses — logic for reconstructing the chain, evidence for auditing the source, scope for noticing what they had not been looking at — are not school exercises the kids do to get good grades. They are the tools the kids used to find out whether they were safe. The story models critical thinking not as cleverness but as care: *this matters, so we are going to think about it properly.*

### A focused-coverage trade-off worth naming

This story declares two social dynamics: `authority_deference` and `group_pressure`. The other dynamic, `conflict_avoidance`, is deliberately absent. These four kids do not avoid conflict — they argue freely with each other, they disagree openly, and their two-year friendship is the kind that can take pushback. Putting conflict-avoidance dynamics on a cast whose whole premise is honest disagreement would fight the story. The `group_pressure` here is not peer conformity pressure; it is the specific, subtler pressure of *not wanting to push back hard on a friend who is visibly scared*. Downstream reviewers and facilitators should treat the absence of `conflict_avoidance` as intentional within this story, not as a gap to patch.

### Alien-anxiety handling (calibration warning)

The alien hypothesis in this story carries real emotional weight for Jules, and by extension for the group. The dialog writer should treat Jules's fear as **uncanny, not horror-adjacent**: the felt content is *something is here and no one is noticing*, never *something will hurt us* or *something will change us*. Adults are *distracted and dismissive*, not hostile or menacing. The fear resolves into *relief*, not *humiliation* — nobody is silly for having been scared, and the story is explicit that the fear was a reasonable response to a genuinely unsettling observation filtered through a credentialed adult's framing. This calibration is the reason `pedagogical_register: unfinished_not_wrong` is declared and the reason `declares_calibration_warnings: true` is on in the frontmatter: the assistive package should lift this warning into the facilitation layer so teachers using the story have language ready for students who land on the fear before they land on the answer.

### Turn-budget rationale

This story's frontmatter declares `turn_budget: "12-18"` and `word_budget: 500`, extending the pipeline's default 10–14 turn range. The rationale is pedagogical: target roughly 60–70% of turns carrying a facet or cognitive-pattern move, with the remaining 30–40% as conversational connective tissue — acknowledgments, transitions, jokes, small clarifications, genuine listening. Every turn being *on* produces artificial dialogue in which no one breathes; the friend-protective `group_pressure` texture in particular needs moments of warmth the kids offer each other between the critical moves, or the dynamic reads as cold. The `transcript_reviewer` for episodes in this story should treat filler turns as a strength, not padding, when they serve cohesion between load-bearing turns. This is a story-level override; `dialog_writer` and `transcript_reviewer` instructions will need to be updated to honor the extended range when Phase 7 runs for this story.
