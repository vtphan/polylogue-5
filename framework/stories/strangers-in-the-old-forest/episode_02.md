## Authorial notes

Episode 2 is where this story becomes a story. Episode 1 established
four kids at a picnic table talking about something strange they saw.
Episode 2 is where the investigation acquires an *authority* — Anya,
via Jules, via Marcus — and the texture of the group shifts with it.
The emotional weight of the scene is Jules's fear surfacing. The
epistemic weight of the scene is the group absorbing a credentialed
voice without auditing it. These happen in the same scene because
they cause each other: the fear is what made Jules call Marcus, the
call to Marcus is what produced Anya, Anya's framing is what Jules
brings back, and the fear is what makes the group handle the framing
gently instead of asking hard questions about it.

The `unresolved_disagreement` shape runs on a three-way tension. Cam
has two moves in this episode — the provenance-check on the TikTok
kid (strength) and the uncritical acceptance of Anya's framing
(weakness) — and the point of having both in the same scene is that
the reader should see them and feel the inconsistency without Cam
seeing it. Maya has started and stopped her objection, so the
disagreement is *present but not voiced*. Jules has landed her fear,
and Priya has offered warmth without arguing. Nothing gets resolved;
nothing gets rejected. The group leaves the picnic table believing
more than they arrived believing, and believing it on a thinner
foundation than they arrived with. That is the shape.

On the `black_and_white_thinking` surfacing now: the reason this
cognitive pattern is held out of episode 1 and planted here is that
the aliens-or-boring framing needs a credentialed voice in the room
to acquire shape. In episode 1 the question is still *what did we
see* — open, exploratory, observational. In episode 2, once Anya's
biosignature framing has entered the group through Jules's retelling,
the question recasts itself in Jules's head as *biosignature or not
biosignature*, which is the specific binary that hides a galaxy of
mundane alternatives (pigmentation genetics, founder effect,
environmental stressors, a thousand things). Jules has not *rejected*
the mundane options — she has not considered them, because the
question no longer has room for them. That is what
`black_and_white_thinking` looks like in a specific head at a
specific moment, and it is different in texture from `false_cause`.
Both patterns can live in the same chain and the episode benefits
from showing both — false_cause from episode 1 is still active
under the surface; black_and_white_thinking is the new addition.

On the `authority_deference` chain: the pattern here is specifically
*authority laundered through intermediaries*. Jules invokes Marcus
invokes Anya invokes a whole research field (astrobiology, which
Marcus studies, which Anya's side work lives at the edge of). At
each hop a caveat falls off, and by the time the authority lands at
the picnic table it is shaped like *my brother's friend, the
researcher, said* — which is lighter and less specific than what
Anya probably actually said, and lighter and less specific than
what Marcus probably passed on, and lighter and less specific than
what Jules heard. The group accepts the laundered version because
Marcus's credibility with Jules vouches for Anya's credibility with
the group, and nobody asks where either credibility comes from.
This is the specific shape of authority_deference that the arc needs
to plant in episode 2 so that episode 8's reversal (Cam calling Anya
himself and asking plain questions) has something to be a reversal
of.

On Maya: this episode now carries a paired weakness for her:
`reasoning_completeness` and a mild early `internal_consistency`
drift. The first move is that she *can feel* the chain has a gap
(her instinct is working fine — she sees it) and she stops short of
finishing the objection aloud. That is specifically a completeness
failure: the chain she would have produced would have asked *who is
Anya? how does Marcus know her? what kind of researcher?* — and she
does not produce it. The second move is that, after sensing the gap,
she lets her spoken position slide toward *Marcus is smart, it's
fine*, which no longer matches the question-first standard she had
just begun to voice. The weakness is intentionally small and
friend-protective, not dramatic. That scale matters because episode
5's repair depends on Maya's drift feeling like a habit she can catch
and correct, not a huge break in character.
She could have produced it; she chose not to. The writing-half-of-it-
in-the-notebook move is load-bearing for episode 5, when Cam reads
an earlier sentence back to her and she revises; the half-objection
from this episode is a candidate for what Cam could read back to
her. The story consistency layer should recognize this setup.

On Priya: no target. Priya is present but not load-bearing in this
episode. Her design-doc identity — U of M biology-faculty mom,
usually-knows-things kid — is in fact the one member of the group
who could most immediately disambiguate *researcher at U of M
working on urban wildlife* (is that biology? is her mom's colleague?
do we know which lab?) and the fact that Priya does not ask that
question is part of the story's patience with 6th graders: Priya
is not on credential-audit duty, she does not see the scene the way
an adult would, she is being a friend to Jules. Her weakness arc
plants in episode 4, not here. Putting a target on her in episode 2
would dilute the episode's focus on authority-via-retelling and would
bend her arc out of shape.

No `black_and_white_thinking` on anyone but Jules in this episode.
No `false_cause` target in this episode (still implicit from ep 1,
but not re-signaled — the episode is about what happens *after* the
chain has been built, when a credentialed voice enters it). These
holsters are deliberate: an establishing-authority scene needs
clarity, not coverage.

## Why these targets

**inferential_validity / Jules / black_and_white_thinking /
authority_deference.** The design doc names Jules's carried facets
as `inferential_validity` and `sufficiency`, her cognitive patterns
as `false_cause` and `black_and_white_thinking`, and her social
dynamic as `authority_deference`. Episode 1 handled `sufficiency` +
`false_cause`. Episode 2 handles `inferential_validity` +
`black_and_white_thinking` + `authority_deference`, because this is
the episode where Jules's chain acquires a credentialed premise and
a binary framing simultaneously. The facet is `inferential_validity`
rather than `sufficiency` because the chain now has links — Anya
said X, Marcus vouches for her, therefore the observation is a Y —
and the gaps are *between* the links, not in the thinness of the
base observation. That is the inferential-validity case.
black_and_white_thinking is the cognitive move that makes the chain
feel closed (the mundane alternatives have been filtered out of the
frame). authority_deference is the social move that makes the chain
feel sanctioned. All three reinforce each other and the episode needs
to show them working together.

**source_credibility / Cam / uncritical_acceptance.** The episode-1
move was uncritical acceptance of a phone-video source; the episode-2
move is uncritical acceptance of a credentialed voice. The design doc
is explicit that Cam's growth is "learning that the phone is a pile
of sources, not a source, *and* learning that the same provenance
question applies to credentialed adults as to creators online." Both
halves of that learning need a concrete starting point, and episode
2 is where the *credentialed* half gets its starting point. The
signal has to land in exactly the same register as yesterday's
TikTok citation — same confidence, same transactional treatment of
*someone said X* as *X is true* — so that the same move can be seen
in two forms and the gap Cam will close in episode 8 is as wide as
possible here.

**reasoning_completeness / Maya / group_pressure (friend-protective).**
The design doc's load-bearing claim about Maya is that she can *feel*
when an argument does not close but has never built the habit of
formalizing why. Episode 2 is the first scene where this feel-it-but-
can't-say-it texture has a *social* reason to stop. Maya in this
episode is not failing to notice a gap; she is noticing one and
choosing, out of care for Jules, not to push on it. That is a
reasoning_completeness weakness with a group_pressure dynamic layered
on top, and the interaction is specifically what the design doc
flagged as "caring about a friend sometimes means pushing back more,
not less." The reversal of this beat — Maya pushing back even when
Jules is scared — is explicitly the arc of episodes 5 and 8. The
half-objection in the notebook is the physical trace the later
episodes can return to.

**source_credibility (strength) / Cam.** The design doc places this
beat in episode 2 (per Cam's character description) and the arc
benefits from placing it here rather than at episode 4. In the same
scene, Cam has both applied and failed to apply the provenance move —
on the TikTok kid he applied it (grudgingly, because his mom pushed
him); on Anya he did not apply it (because Jules sounded confident
and the word *researcher* did the work). Putting both in the same
scene lets the unresolved_disagreement shape end on a specific
reader experience: "he *can* do this move, and he *didn't* do it on
the credential." That reader experience is what the whole Cam arc
will return to. An earlier-than-ep-4 placement of this strength also
gives Cam's ep-8 self-initiated Anya call more runway to feel like
the growth it is meant to be.

## Calibration note for this episode

This is the episode where Jules's fear becomes audible. The design
doc's calibration warning — *uncanny, not horror-adjacent* —
applies most strongly here. Jules's *what if it's happening and
no one's noticing* must land as isolation-and-anxiety, not as
threat-of-harm. The pressure on Maya to soften her objection is
*care*, not *conformity*. When Priya offers warmth at the end of
the scene, it is *presence*, not *agreement* — the friendship is
the container the fear can live in, not a group decision to
believe what Jules believes. The dialog writer and transcript
reviewer should treat these distinctions as load-bearing.
