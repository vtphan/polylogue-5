# Saving the Maker Space

## Premise

The principal has announced that the school's maker space — a cluttered back room full of soldering irons, sewing machines, a 3D printer that mostly works, and a couch nobody is supposed to sit on — will close at the end of the term. The room is being converted into a second computer lab. The five 6th graders who spend most of their lunches and after-school time in the maker space form an ad-hoc club to fight the decision. They investigate why the closure is happening, write a petition, present it, lose, regroup, and eventually negotiate a partial reprieve: the maker space survives, but in a smaller form, on a schedule shared with two other clubs.

The story is about how a group with the right instincts and the wrong evidence learns to argue better — and about how winning a little is sometimes what good reasoning earns you, instead of winning everything.

## Setting

A public middle school in a midsize town. Generic enough to travel: it could be any school where the principal is balancing a budget and the kids are losing a room they care about. The maker space itself is specific in its details (the 3D printer that jams on the third layer, the box of broken headphones nobody throws out, the corkboard covered in failed prototypes) because those details are what makes it worth saving. The principal is a real character, not a villain — she has reasons, she will explain them when asked, and the kids will spend most of the story underestimating how good her reasons are.

The action happens in lunch periods, after-school meetings in the maker space itself, one tense meeting in the principal's office, and one classroom where the group regroups after the petition fails. No adults narrate; the kids talk to each other and to the principal directly.

## Cast

There are five recurring characters. Each episode features two or three of them as leads. All five are 6th graders who have known each other since elementary school but are not all close friends — the maker space is what brought them together, and the fight to save it is the first time they've had to coordinate as a group with stakes.

### Mira

Mira is the organizer. She is the one who decides on the first day that the group exists, sets the meeting time, brings the notebook, and writes "PETITION" at the top of the first page. She runs on conviction and she is good at it — when Mira is right about strategy, the group moves fast and confidently, and the story would not exist without her.

Her weakness is that she treats how settled a question *feels* as evidence that it *is* settled. She reads one article on her phone the night the announcement drops — a decade-old NPR piece about hands-on learning — and treats it as the whole record. When Theo asks for the budget number she's citing, she can't produce it, but she's already said "the principal has to know this," which makes the missing source feel like the principal's problem to defend instead of theirs to substantiate. When the group is moving toward a decision, Mira is the one who states the consensus a beat too early — "okay so we all agree we're doing the petition, right?" — and once she has said it, dissenting feels like breaking the group rather than completing the conversation.

Mira does not have a growth arc. By the end of the story she is still the kid who states the consensus too early; what changes is that the group around her has learned to push back on it. That is the story's argument: you do not need to fix Mira to fix the group, and Mira is not the problem to solve.

She carries `false_certainty` as a cognitive pattern across the season and contributes to `group_pressure` as a social dynamic. Her stable strength is `relevance` in episode 5 — when the compromise is on the table, she is the one who keeps the group focused on what would actually persuade the principal, instead of what would feel best to say.

### Theo

Theo is the hesitator. He asks "are we sure?" before every decision, which is sometimes the most useful thing anyone says and sometimes the most paralyzing. His instinct is to look for the hole in any argument, including arguments he agrees with. In episode 1 he is the one who asks where Mira's NPR article actually came from; he is also the one who, after asking, lets Mira move on without an answer because he doesn't want to be the kid who slows everything down.

His weakness is the inverse of confirmation bias: he discounts evidence that supports the group's position because he is primed to find holes, and so he ends up trusting arguments he disagrees with more than arguments he agrees with. This is `confirmation_bias` in the same family as Mira's pattern but pulling in the opposite direction. When the principal explains the budget, Theo is the first one in the group to take her seriously — partly because she is right, and partly because she is contradicting the group, and contradicting the group is where Theo's attention naturally goes.

His stable strength is `source_credibility` — he is the one who, when given a source, will actually try to figure out where it comes from and whether to trust it. He carries this strength most visibly in episode 3, when the petition's evidence base finally gets audited and his caution turns out to have been pointing at something real all along.

The moment of surprise in this story belongs to Theo. In episode 3, when the petition fails and the group expects him to say "I told you so," he instead refuses to let them blame the principal. "She has a point," he says, more firmly than he says anything else in the season. "What did we actually offer her?" That is in-character — Theo's caution has always been about getting things right, not about being right — but it is unexpected because the group has built him into the brake, and brakes are not supposed to push the car forward.

Theo does not have a growth arc either. He stays cautious; what changes is that the group learns when to listen to his caution instead of working around it.

### Dev

Dev is the charismatic frontman. He talks fast, gestures a lot, and has the kind of confidence that makes other 6th graders want to agree with him before they have figured out whether they do. He is the one who can actually deliver a presentation when the moment requires it, which is why the petition presentation in episode 2 is his to give — and which is also why the petition is so persuasive in tone and so hollow in substance.

His weakness is two facets at once. First, `relevance`: Dev charges ahead with whatever framing sounds compelling in the moment, regardless of whether it actually addresses what the principal would care about. The petition argues that the maker space is "essential to learning" — true and unfalsifiable, which is exactly the problem. Second, `inferential_validity`: Dev's arguments do not always follow from his premises. "The maker space matters because the kids love it" feels like a complete argument when Dev says it; it is not. He is the cast member who most consistently exhibits the gap between an argument that performs well and an argument that holds up.

Dev does not contribute much to the social-dynamic side of the story directly, but his confidence is part of what makes Mira's premature consensus statements stick — when Dev nods, the group nods, and once the group has nodded the conversation has moved on. So `group_pressure` runs through both Mira and Dev, with Mira as the speaker and Dev as the amplifier.

His stable strength is the one the cast cannot do without: when the group has finally figured out *what* to say, Dev is the one who can walk into the principal's office and actually say it. Episode 5's compromise proposal lands because Dev can deliver it.

Dev does not grow. His strengths and weaknesses are both real and the story needs both.

### Sam

Sam is the quiet one. She listens more than she talks, and when she disagrees with the group she usually keeps it to herself. Her social default is `conflict_avoidance` — not because she lacks opinions but because she has watched, since elementary school, what happens to kids who break the consensus in a room where Mira is talking, and she does not want to be one of those kids. In episodes 1, 2, and 3 she is in the room for every important decision and contributes almost nothing to the record.

Sam has the story's major growth arc. In episode 4, after the petition fails and the group is trying to figure out what went wrong, Sam realizes — and forces herself to say — that the petition assumed every kid in the school used the maker space, when in fact only about half of them ever set foot in it. The petition spoke for "the kids" when it actually spoke for "us." That is `perspective_engagement` as a strength: noticing whose voice is missing, and being able to name it. It is also, structurally, the moment Sam grows out of her own conflict avoidance — because to say it she has to interrupt Mira, and she does.

Episode 5 shows Sam holding the new ground but uncomfortably. She speaks up in the principal's office — once, briefly, on the question of who the new shared schedule should accommodate. She does not become a different person. Growth in this story is real but partial: Sam ends the season as a kid who *can* speak up when the stakes are high enough, not as a kid who reliably does.

Her stable strength after the inflection is `perspective_engagement`. Before the inflection she has no carried strength — she is in the background. That is intentional: growth carriers earn their strength by inflecting, and Sam earns hers in episode 4.

### Ren

Ren is the researcher. She is genuinely curious, the only kid in the group who actually goes looking for evidence between meetings, and the one most likely to walk into a session saying "okay so I read this thing." Her problem is that she has no consistent way of telling a great primary source from an obviously biased blog post, and she treats everything she finds as roughly equivalent input.

Her weakness in episodes 1–2 is `source_credibility` — not as a character flaw exactly, but as a skill she has not been taught. In episode 1 she brings three "studies" to the group; one is a peer-reviewed paper on hands-on learning, one is a parent advocacy blog, one is a press release from a 3D-printer manufacturer. To Ren they are three pieces of evidence pointing in the same direction. To Theo (who is the one who eventually looks at them) they are not.

Ren has the smaller of the two growth arcs. Episode 3's failure shakes her enough that she starts asking *where* a source comes from before treating it as input — not consistently, but more often. Episodes 4 and 5 show her doing this unevenly. Her growth is smaller than Sam's in dramatic weight but real: by episode 5 she is the kid who, when Dev is about to cite something in the principal's office, says "wait, where did we get that?" and the group listens.

Ren is also Scope-leaning in her instincts. She is the one who tends to ask what the bigger picture is, even when the bigger picture is uncomfortable for the group's preferred conclusion. That instinct is what eventually lets her partner with Sam in episode 4 — Ren is the one who can ask "okay, so who are we leaving out?" once Sam has named that someone is being left out.

After her inflection, Ren contributes to the group's `relevance` strength: she is part of why the compromise proposal in episode 5 actually addresses what the principal cares about, instead of what the group cares about.

## Arc summary

The five episodes trace a single argument through five phases. Episode 1 is the announcement landing and the group forming around the wrong idea. Episode 2 is the petition: the group commits to a plan that they have not pressure-tested, and Mira's premature consensus and Dev's persuasive shallowness combine to ship something that sounds good and is not good enough. Episode 3 is the principal's response, which is calm and unexpectedly persuasive, and the group's first real reckoning with the possibility that they were wrong about the evidence even though they were right about the cause. Episode 4 is the regrouping: Sam breaks her silence, the group realizes they were arguing for "the kids" when they meant "us," and they have to rebuild the proposal around a question they had not asked. Episode 5 is the compromise: a redesigned proposal, a partial win, and an ending nobody is fully happy about — including the kids.

Across the five episodes the lens dispositions rotate. Episode 1 is evidence-primary (the question is whether the group has a basis for what they're claiming). Episode 2 is evidence-primary (the petition's evidence base is the central problem). Episode 3 is logic-primary (the principal's argument is structurally sound and the group has to engage with it on its own terms). Episodes 4 and 5 are scope-primary (who is missing, and what does the proposal actually have to address). No lens dominates; every lens carries at least one episode.

The mixed-valence shapes also rotate: an early-strength-collapse opening (episode 1), an unresolved disagreement (episode 2), a strength-prevails turn (episode 3, where Theo's caution finally lands), a self-correction (episode 4, where Sam names what's been missing), and a stalemate (episode 5, where the win and the loss are both real and neither cancels the other).

## Stakes

What the kids stand to lose is concrete: a room where they have spent hundreds of hours, where they know which drawer the wire strippers are in, where the broken-headphones box is. They also stand to lose each other, in a softer sense — the maker space is the reason this group exists, and without it they are five kids who happen to share a hallway.

What they stand to gain is harder to name and is the real subject of the story. They gain — partially, unevenly, not all of them, not all the time — the ability to argue for something they care about in a way that takes the other side seriously, that asks who they are not speaking for, and that survives contact with a budget. They also gain a partial reprieve, which is a smaller thing than they wanted and a more honest thing than they expected.

The story's ending earns its lack of tidy resolution. The maker space survives, but in a smaller form, on a schedule shared with two other clubs whose members the group did not previously think of as part of the conversation. Some of what they loved about the room is gone. Some of what they were doing wrong is still wrong. They did not unambiguously win, and the story refuses to pretend they did.

## Pedagogical commitments

This story is built around four things students should walk away noticing.

**One: arguments that feel right are not the same as arguments that hold up.** Mira's premature consensus and Dev's persuasive shallowness are both versions of this, and the petition's failure in episode 3 is the moment the story commits to it. The principal is right, partially, and the group has to learn what it feels like to be partially wrong without being entirely wrong.

**Two: caution is a contribution, not a brake.** Theo is the cast member students will most easily underestimate, and the story is structured so that by episode 3 his caution has become the most valuable thing in the room. The moment of surprise — Theo refusing to let the group blame the principal — is the story's clearest commitment to this.

**Three: who you are arguing for is part of the argument.** Sam's growth in episode 4 is not about confidence; it is about realizing that the petition spoke for a "we" that excluded half the school, and that fixing this is more important than holding the group's existing consensus together. Perspective engagement is the story's pedagogical center.

**Four: good reasoning produces progress, not victory.** The compromise in episode 5 is the deliberate refusal of a tidy ending. The story is not "the kids learn to reason and then they win." It is "the kids learn to reason and then they earn a partial thing that nobody fully likes, and that is what learning to reason gets you."
