# The Field Trip

## Premise

Ms. Tsai's 6th grade Integrated Studies class gets one field trip this year. The class's whole idea is asking questions that cut across subjects — how the world actually works, not how textbooks divide it up. A five-person student committee has been asked to propose both the subject and the destination, and to submit a written recommendation that Ms. Tsai will evaluate on its reasoning quality, not its conclusion. If the reasoning is sound, the class goes wherever the committee recommends. If the reasoning is weak, Ms. Tsai will use the recommendation as a teaching moment and pick the destination herself.

Each committee member walks in with a preference they haven't examined. The preferences came first; the educational justifications come second, constructed backward to make the preferred destination look like the smart choice. As the committee argues across five meetings, their real reasons start showing through the careful arguments. One member proposed the right kind of answer in the first meeting — a genuinely cross-disciplinary question about something the class encounters every day — and nobody took her seriously.

The story is about motivated reasoning: what happens when you build an argument to justify a conclusion you already reached, and how that habit makes you unable to recognize honest reasoning when you see it. The committee doesn't cleanly resolve this. Their final recommendation is better than their first drafts but still shows the fingerprints of the preferences that shaped it, and Ms. Tsai's feedback at the end names exactly what she saw.

## Setting

A public middle school in a midsize town. The committee meets during a weekly Integrated Studies block in their regular classroom, sometimes in the library when they need to look things up. The class has a whiteboard where they track proposals, and a shared document where they're building the recommendation. The teacher, Ms. Tsai, is present but deliberately non-directive — she answers direct questions honestly but will not steer the committee toward a conclusion. She has told them her evaluation criteria up front: "I'm reading your reasoning, not your answer."

The school is generic enough to travel. The lunch program is run by a regional food service company (Greenleaf Dining) that operates out of a central kitchen twelve miles from the school. The science museum is downtown and charges $18 per student plus transport. The zoo is forty minutes away with a group rate. The amusement park is an hour away and charges $35 per student. Downtown is walkable from the school.

## Cast

Five 6th graders on the field trip committee. They volunteered or were drafted; they are not all friends. What they share is that they are all in Ms. Tsai's Integrated Studies class and they all care, in different ways, about where this trip goes — some because of the destination, some because of the subject, some because they want to look good on the committee.

### Sam

Sam is quiet and methodical. She listens more than she talks, and when she does talk she tends to ask questions that reframe the conversation — "wait, what are we actually trying to learn?" — that the others find either clarifying or annoying depending on how committed they are to their current argument. She is Logic-leaning in a structural sense: she cares about whether the group is answering the question it was asked, not a different question it would prefer.

Sam's proposal is the one nobody takes seriously: she suggests the committee study how the school's own lunch program works — where the food comes from, who decides the menu, what happens when students can't pay — and visit Greenleaf Dining's central kitchen. The subject is genuinely cross-disciplinary (touching science, civics, economics, equity, logistics), which makes it the only proposal that directly answers what Integrated Studies is for. The destination is unglamorous, and the other committee members dismiss it as "not a real field trip." Sam does not push hard. She makes her case once, clearly, and when it's dismissed she participates in the rest of the discussion without sulking but also without abandoning her position.

Sam does not have a growth arc. She starts the story reasoning honestly and ends the story still reasoning honestly. Her function is the epistemic anchor: the character whose honest proposal is the thing the others can't see because motivated reasoning makes them assume everyone is rationalizing.

Her stable strength is `relevance` — she consistently asks whether the argument addresses the actual question. In one episode she also carries `condition_sensitivity` as a strength, asking the conditional question nobody else thinks to ask: "under what conditions *would* the amusement park actually be the right choice?" — reframing a flat rejection into a testable claim. These strengths make it painful when the committee dismisses her proposal — the reader can see she's right, even if the committee can't.

### Priya

Priya is articulate, confident, and usually the most careful thinker in any group. She is Scope-leaning: in most contexts she is the first to ask whose perspective is missing, who benefits from a decision, who is left out. Her Integrated Studies classmates know her for this and respect it.

Her proposal is the science museum — specifically, studying how science gets communicated to the public, with the museum as a case study. The proposal is well-constructed and sounds rigorous. What Priya has not examined is why she proposed it: her mother is the education director at the museum, which means Priya grew up visiting for free, knows the staff by name, and genuinely believes the museum is the best destination in town for any learning purpose. The proposal is motivated reasoning dressed in Priya's strongest voice.

The museum is also expensive: $18 per student plus transport. Priya's family can afford destinations like this without thinking about it, and she has not thought about it. This is the core of her failure: the kid who is best at asking "whose perspective is missing?" cannot see that her own financial position is a perspective, and that classmates who can't afford $18 are the ones whose perspective she's missing.

Priya has the story's single growth arc. Pre-growth (episodes 1–3), she advocates confidently, deflects cost concerns ("there are scholarship programs"), and does not notice the irony. The inflection comes in episode 4, when a specific moment makes the cost personal — she overhears or is told something she can't rationalize away. Post-growth (episode 5), Priya does not smoothly pivot. She withdraws her proposal, but the withdrawal is awkward and uncomfortable: she has to sit with having been the scope-blind character for three episodes, and the rest of the committee doesn't quite know how to respond because they haven't confronted their own motivated reasoning yet. Priya's recognition exposes the group's pattern without the group being ready for it.

She carries `egocentric_thinking` as a cognitive pattern (seeing only from her own position) and contributes to `authority_deference` as a social dynamic (subtly invoking her mother's expertise to bolster her case). Pre-growth, `perspective_engagement` is her ironic weakness: the faculty she's known for fails on the one case where the perspective she needs to take is her own. Post-growth (episode 5), `perspective_engagement` returns as a genuine strength — the payoff of the arc. The irony is that her strength and her weakness are the same faculty, applied to different targets at different points in the story.

### Maya

Maya is fast-talking, enthusiastic, and builds arguments that sound convincing in the moment but don't hold up under scrutiny. She is Logic-leaning: she cares about whether an argument has a structure, and she's good at making one. Her weakness is that she's too good at it — she can construct a logical-sounding chain from any premise to any conclusion, and she doesn't always notice when the chain has gaps.

Her proposal is the amusement park — specifically, studying the physics of motion using roller coasters as real-world laboratories. Her argument: "roller coasters are applied physics, physics is a real subject, seeing it in real life is better than reading about it in a book, therefore the amusement park is the most educational option." The argument has a structure. The structure has gaps. "Applied physics" is a real thing; "the amusement park is the best place to learn applied physics for an Integrated Studies class" is a leap that skips several questions, including whether the class is studying physics at all.

Maya does not have a growth arc. By the end of the story she is still the kid who builds confident inferential chains with gaps. What changes is that the committee's discussion makes those gaps visible to the reader, even when the committee itself doesn't fully confront them. Maya is not wrong to be enthusiastic; she is wrong to treat enthusiasm as evidence.

She carries `confirmation_bias` (filtering information toward her preferred conclusion), `inferential_validity` errors (the leaps between her premises), and `condition_sensitivity` as a weakness (she treats "roller coasters involve physics" as unconditional without asking what would have to be true — a structured worksheet, a physics teacher present, time to actually measure something — for the visit to teach physics). The distinction between Maya's `inferential_validity` weakness and her `sufficiency` weakness matters: `inferential_validity` is "the argument leaps from A to C" (the chain has a gap); `sufficiency` is "the argument only has A and nothing else" (the chain doesn't have enough premises). Both show up in different episodes: `inferential_validity` in her initial pitch (ep 1–2), `sufficiency` in her alliance-building (ep 3) where the argument thins further as she compromises with Kai.

She contributes to `group_pressure` — Maya's confidence is socially contagious, and when she's arguing for the amusement park, the energy in the room shifts toward her even when the argument is weak. Her stable strength is `inferential_validity` in the episodes where she turns her chain-building skill toward *questioning* someone else's argument — she is just as good at finding gaps in others' reasoning as she is at hiding the gaps in her own.

### Jordan

Jordan is earnest, well-intentioned, and takes information at face value. He is Evidence-leaning: he wants data, sources, things you can point to. His weakness is that he doesn't interrogate the sources he finds — if something looks official, he trusts it.

His proposal is the zoo — specifically, studying animal behavior and ecology. He volunteered at the zoo last summer and loved it. His argument leans on the zoo's marketing materials: "the zoo has state-certified educational programs, they're aligned with Next Generation Science Standards, and they have a dedicated school group coordinator." All of this is true. None of it means the zoo is the right destination for this class's question. "State-certified" is a real credential; Jordan has not asked what the certification actually certifies or whether it applies to Integrated Studies rather than biology.

Jordan does not have a growth arc. He ends the story still trusting official-sounding credentials, though the committee's discussion has made his tendency visible. Jordan is not gullible — he is *trusting*, and the story respects the difference while showing where trust without verification leads.

He carries `uncritical_acceptance` as a cognitive pattern and contributes to `authority_deference` (treating official credentials as settling the question). His stable strength is `source_credibility` in the episodes where he is the one who actually looks something up rather than citing it from memory — when Jordan does the work, he's good at it. The problem is that he often doesn't do the work.

### Kai

Kai is pragmatic, impatient with abstraction, and focuses on logistics. He is not aligned with any particular lens disposition — his reasoning signature is "what's practical?" which cuts across all three lenses but also cuts across the *question being asked*, because Kai treats practical constraints as if they were the question.

His proposal is a walking tour of downtown — studying urban planning by observing buildings, traffic patterns, public spaces, and businesses within walking distance of the school. His real motivation is that it's free and close: no bus rental, no admission fee, back before lunch. His subject ("urban planning") is the one he thought of second, after the destination; the subject is tailored to the walk, not the walk to the subject.

Kai does not have a growth arc. He is the story's pragmatist throughout, and his pragmatism serves an important structural function: Kai is the character who surfaces the cost issue that triggers Priya's inflection, not because he cares about equity but because he's tracking the budget. His line — something like "the museum costs eighteen bucks a person, the bus is another twelve, that's thirty dollars times twenty-eight students" — is motivated by logistics, not empathy. But it's the line that makes Priya flinch, because the number is concrete in a way that "some students can't afford it" is not.

He carries `tunnel_vision` (his pragmatic frame crowds out the educational question) and `relevance` errors (treating practical considerations as if they answered the educational question). He contributes to `conflict_avoidance` in a specific way: Kai avoids arguments not because he's afraid of conflict but because arguments are inefficient, and his "let's just move on" energy suppresses productive disagreement. His stable strength is `consequence_consideration` — when the committee is lost in abstract arguments, Kai is the one who asks "okay but what actually happens if we pick this one?"

## Arc across episodes

### Episode 1 — The Proposals

The committee's first meeting. Ms. Tsai has told them the criteria (reasoning, not conclusion) and the class's purpose (cross-disciplinary questions). Each member presents their proposal. Sam's lunch-program proposal is presented clearly and dismissed quickly — "that's not a field trip, that's the cafeteria." The episode ends with four proposals on the whiteboard and Sam's crossed out. The committee agrees to "research their options" before the next meeting, which in practice means each member will look for evidence that supports their own proposal.

### Episode 2 — The Evidence

Each committee member brings evidence. Jordan has the zoo's brochure. Maya has found a YouTube video of a physics teacher at an amusement park. Priya has a printout from the museum's education page. Kai has a downtown walking map with buildings labeled. Sam brings a set of questions she wrote down: "Who decides the school lunch menu? Where does the food come from? How much does it cost per student per day? What happens if a family can't pay?" Nobody engages with Sam's questions because they are not arguments for a destination — they are the kind of questions Integrated Studies is supposed to ask, but the committee has already moved past asking questions and into defending answers. The evidence each person brought is shaped by the conclusion they started with.

### Episode 3 — The Alliances

The committee starts forming blocs. Maya and Kai discover they can form a tactical alliance: Maya's amusement park is fun and Kai's walking tour is cheap, so they start negotiating a compromise ("what if we do a walking tour that ends at the amusement park?") that satisfies both their preferences but doesn't answer the educational question at all. Priya and Jordan form an implicit "serious institution" bloc — museum vs. zoo is at least a competition between educational destinations, which makes both of them look reasonable compared to the amusement-park-walking-tour coalition. Sam is isolated between the blocs, still asking her questions, increasingly frustrated. This is the episode where Sam asks a `condition_sensitivity` question — "under what conditions *would* the amusement park actually teach us something?" — that Maya deflects and nobody follows up on.

The social dynamics are fully loaded: `group_pressure` from the blocs, `conflict_avoidance` from Sam (who stops pushing rather than fight two alliances), `authority_deference` from Jordan (citing credentials) and Priya (invoking institutional prestige). But the bloc-formation arguments themselves carry facet-level weaknesses, not just social-dynamic content: the Maya-Kai compromise is a `relevance` failure (they've abandoned the educational question entirely for a logistical deal), the Priya-Jordan "serious institution" frame relies on institutional prestige as evidence (`source_credibility` weakness — prestige is not the same as educational fit), and both blocs treat agreement-between-two-people as if it constituted evidence for the quality of the argument (`sufficiency` weakness — two people agreeing doesn't make the argument stronger, it just makes the coalition larger).

### Episode 4 — The Number

The inflection episode. Kai, doing his usual budget math for the next meeting, says something concrete about the museum's cost: the per-student total with transport. The number lands differently than the abstraction did. Someone — possibly Sam, possibly Kai in a follow-up — mentions that six students in the class are on the free lunch program. Priya's first reaction is defensive ("there are scholarship programs at the museum"). Her second reaction, a beat later, is quieter: she realizes she has never checked whether the scholarship programs cover field trips, and she realizes she didn't check because she didn't need to. The scope failure she would have caught in anyone else's argument is one she built into her own.

This is also the episode where Sam's proposal resurfaces — not because Sam pushes it, but because the cost conversation forces the committee to reconsider what "a good field trip" means. The lunch-program questions Sam raised in episode 2 suddenly look different: they were about the school's own systems, which are exactly the kind of cross-disciplinary question the class is supposed to ask, and the destination is free. The committee doesn't pivot to Sam's proposal (the momentum is too far along and the blocs are still operating), but the question is on the table.

### Episode 5 — The Recommendation

The committee has to write and submit the recommendation. Priya has withdrawn her museum proposal but doesn't know what to support instead. Maya is still arguing for the amusement park. Jordan is holding onto the zoo but with less conviction. Kai wants to submit anything and be done. Sam restates her proposal one more time, plainly.

The final recommendation is a compromise that shows the fingerprints of the process: it doesn't fully commit to any one option and its reasoning is better than episode 1 but still shows the shape of the preferences that built it. The episode 5 transcript covers only the committee's final debate about what to write in the recommendation. Ms. Tsai is not a speaking character in any transcript — her feedback happens after the discussion ends and is described in the episode's prose body as a post-discussion beat, not staged as transcript turns. Her comment — "You started from your answers and worked backward to your questions. One of you started from the question. I could tell" — is referenced through framing (the `consensus_check` for this episode can ask: "Ms. Tsai read your recommendation. What do you think she noticed?"), not through dialog. The story ends without resolving whether the class actually goes on the trip or where they go — the point is the reasoning, and the reasoning was the thing being evaluated all along.

This is also the episode where Priya's `perspective_engagement` returns as a genuine strength, post-growth: she is the one who, in the final debate, asks whose perspective the recommendation is missing — and this time the question is honest because she has already confronted the version of it that applied to herself.

## Stakes

The immediate stakes are practical: the class goes on a field trip, and the committee's reasoning determines whether they pick the destination or the teacher does. But the real stakes are epistemic and social. The epistemic stakes: can the committee recognize its own motivated reasoning before submitting a recommendation that an adult will see through? The social stakes: can the committee members maintain their relationships when confronting each other's (and their own) rationalizations is what good reasoning requires?

The story does not resolve tidily. Priya's growth is real but uncomfortable. Maya's inferential leaps are visible but unconfronted. Jordan's trust in credentials is noted but not corrected. Kai's pragmatism accidentally did the right thing for the wrong reason. Sam's honest reasoning was right all along but was never adopted. Ms. Tsai's closing feedback is the mirror the committee needed and didn't build for itself.

## Pedagogical commitments

This story teaches motivated reasoning — specifically, the habit of constructing an argument backward from a preferred conclusion, and how that habit blinds you to honest reasoning when you encounter it. The v2 assistive package should find rich material here:

- **Afforded-missing cells** are structural: the committee repeatedly fails to ask the questions that would undermine their preferences (what is Integrated Studies actually for? what does "state-certified" certify? who can't afford $18?). These are lenses richly afforded by the text but not engaged by the characters.
- **Social-cognitive interaction** is the premise: `confirmation_bias` is amplified by `group_pressure` (alliances reinforce shared rationalizations); `egocentric_thinking` is amplified by `authority_deference` (Priya's mother's expertise makes her position seem objective); `uncritical_acceptance` is amplified by `authority_deference` (Jordan's trust in official credentials).
- **Discrimination surfaces** are rich: a student might flag `source_credibility` when the real problem is `relevance` (the zoo's credentials are real but irrelevant to Integrated Studies), or flag `inferential_validity` when the real problem is `sufficiency` (Maya's argument has a structure, but the structure doesn't have enough premises).
- **Continuation chains** into the group phase are natural: a student who notices "the committee never asked what Integrated Studies is actually for" has exactly the right private insight to bring to a group discussion.
- **Perspective transitions** are available in every passage: each committee member is arguing from their own lens, so cross-lens moves are always possible.
- **Counterfactuals** are concrete and turn-specific: "Maya could have asked 'does our class study physics?' before building her amusement-park argument" is a specific fix at a specific turn.
