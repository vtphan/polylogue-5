# The Overton Park Sightings

## Premise

Five 6th graders in Memphis keep seeing white squirrels in Overton Park. One of them — Jules, who has grown up on her mom's weird-science articles and her older brother's astrobiology enthusiasm — is convinced the sightings are evidence of something unusual. The other four don't believe it, but they don't have a better answer either, and the sightings are real. Over eight episodes the group investigates: they count squirrels, they talk to a zookeeper, they learn what genotype and phenotype mean, they plot sightings on a map of the park, they discover they've been walking only the south side, they walk the north side, they do the probability math, and they end up at a plain but satisfying answer — recessive alleles in a founder-limited park population — that dissolves the alien hypothesis without humiliating the kid who brought it.

The story is about how *thin reasoning is not wrong reasoning* — it is reasoning that hasn't finished yet. Every character starts the season with a genuinely weak move, and every growth carrier earns their revision on-screen by doing more work: asking a better question, writing the steps down, auditing a source, walking somewhere they haven't walked. The story's pedagogical position is that the right response to a thin argument is not "you're wrong" but "show me more," and that characters who begin the season with weak facet values are not bad thinkers — they're working thinkers who haven't done the next step yet.

## Setting

Overton Park in Memphis, Tennessee — the Old Forest, the Greensward, the zoo adjacent, Rhodes College a few blocks north. Real place, real squirrels, real 131 acres of urban forest that 6th graders actually bike to after school. The park is specific in its details (the shelter by the playground, the path that loops the forest, the fence along the zoo's back) because the mystery depends on place: which parts of the park the kids walk, and which parts they don't, is the whole scope question.

The five kids go to the same middle school in Midtown Memphis and meet at the park after school. Home locations are specific enough to matter to the story — Jules in Cooper-Young, Maya in South Memphis, Cam in Binghampton, Priya in East Memphis, Tye in Frayser — because which part of Memphis each kid comes from shapes what they know and what they don't. The action happens at the park, briefly inside the zoo's keeper area (one scene, one episode), at an outdoor picnic table where they spread out their notebooks, and never inside anyone's home. The only adult who ever has speaking turns in a transcript is the zookeeper in episode 3, and only in that episode. All other adults — Jules's mother, Cam's mother, Priya's mother, Tye's older sister — are referenced in the kids' dialogue but never speak on-screen themselves. The five kids know each other's neighborhoods by name from two years of school talk, and that matters structurally: when one of them generalizes from their own part of Memphis, the others have the raw material to notice it even when they don't always use it.

## Cast

There are five recurring characters. Each episode features two or three of them as leads; one episode (the final) features all five. The five are 6th graders who go to the same school but come from five different parts of Memphis, and that spread is not incidental — it is part of how the story's scope problem gets solved.

### Jules

Jules is the engine. She is the reason the investigation exists. On the first day of the season she tells the others she saw a white squirrel in the Old Forest and she *means it* — not as a joke, not as a gambit for attention, but as a real claim she wants the group to take seriously. Her mom is a freelance science writer who makes a living writing curious-weird-science pieces for kids' magazines, the kind of mom who shows you a video of tardigrades at breakfast and quotes papers about extremophiles during long car rides. Jules's older brother Marcus is a sophomore at Rhodes College studying astrobiology and used to walk her through the Old Forest explaining what SETI was looking for. She has grown up on a worldview where anomalies matter and the boring explanation is sometimes the lazy one. That is a *real epistemology* and the story treats it that way.

Her weakness is `false_cause`. When Jules sees something weird her mind pattern-matches on surface similarity: white squirrel looks like a mutation, mutations come from something, maybe something we don't know about yet. The chain feels tight to her because her mother's articles are full of exactly this kind of chain — but her mother's articles also do the work Jules has skipped, which is asking what would have to be true for each link to hold. Jules has absorbed Marcus's conclusions and her mother's enthusiasm without absorbing their process, and her growth across the season is learning to complete the chain rather than abandon it.

Jules does not carry a lens-growth arc in the formal sense. Her arc is meta: by episode 8 she can formulate her alien hypothesis in the falsifiable form her mother's best articles take — "aliens would predict the sightings are getting more frequent and spreading outward; genetics predicts they're stable and clustered; the data fits genetics" — and concede honestly that the data fits the mundane explanation. That concession is not a loss. It is the moment her mother's epistemology finally *works for* her reasoning instead of decorating it. Her cognitive pattern shifts from `false_cause` as a standalone move toward a more disciplined version of the same instinct — *take the weird seriously, then rule out the mundane first*. Her episode-8 reframe is also a Logic-lens beat in its own right: the falsifiable reformulation is a move on `inferential_validity`, and the rubric should be able to touch it even though her primary arc is meta.

Her carried facets are `inferential_validity` (the leap-of-cause chain in episode 1) and `sufficiency` — she treats a single sighting as the basis for a whole hypothesis, and that is the purest form of thin reasoning the story has. Pattern and facet pair cleanly: the false-cause chain runs on a base of one observation, and the sufficiency weakness and the false-cause pattern are two surfaces of the same episode-1 move.

Jules also carries `authority_deference` in two episodes. In episode 3 she accepts the zookeeper's framing of genotype versus phenotype the same way she accepts her mother's — the episode is designed so a careful reader can notice it is the same move wearing a different hat, and the pairing with her `uncritical_acceptance` is the whole point. In episode 7, while the group is doing the probability math, Jules cites the zookeeper's earlier explanation as settled truth, and Cam — the season's Evidence grower — is the one who pushes back and asks where the zookeeper got the number. That second appearance is what turns `authority_deference` from a one-episode cameo into a real through-line for Jules, and it is also the season's clearest demonstration that Cam's Evidence growth now transfers to auditing other kids' claims and not only his own.

### Maya

Maya is the Logic grower. Her dad is a pastor at a small neighborhood church in South Memphis and her mom works in logistics operations at FedEx, and Maya has been around arguments her whole life — sermons that had to land, family dinners where her mom walked through why a delivery route was wrong. She can *feel* when an argument works. What she has never been asked to do is formalize why. Her instinct is strong enough that nobody pushes her to explain, and so she has never built the muscle. She says things like "that doesn't sound right" and "that feels off" and is usually correct, which is the problem.

Her weakness is `false_certainty`. This is the same pattern Mira in *Saving the Maker Space* carried, but it lives in a very different place. Mira's version was about group consensus — she stated the group's direction a beat too early and treated it as settled. Maya's version is about individual intuition — she treats the feeling of being right as sufficient grounds for being right. Mira never grew. Maya does.

Maya's growth trajectory runs across three episodes: she is visibly weak in episode 3 (the zookeeper scene, where she cannot reconstruct the inferential chain from recessive allele to observable frequency even though she can feel it should work), she inflects in episode 4 (where she writes the chain out step by step in her notebook for the first time in her life, clunky and slow, and catches her own bad inference mid-writing), and she confirms the growth in episode 7 (where she constructs a second chain faster and cleaner, on base rates and founder effects). By episode 8 she is the kid who formalizes things without being asked, and she does it because she has learned that her father's rule — *show your work in a sermon or nobody trusts you* — applies to her own thinking as well as to preaching.

Maya is one of the two characters whose growth is most visible to a 6th-grade reader because her inflection is physical: she writes on-page, and the writing is the turn. Her carried facets are `inferential_validity` and `reasoning_completeness`, both as weaknesses in episodes 3 and 4 and as growing strengths in episodes 7 and 8. Lens: Logic.

### Cam

Cam is the Evidence grower. He lives in Binghampton. His mom is an ER nurse at Methodist; his dad works construction. Cam had a phone earlier than the other kids — partly because his parents' schedules meant he was unsupervised more in the evenings — and he spends the most time of anyone in the group on social media. His information environment is a scroll: a TikTok, a news clip, a meme, a friend's cousin, all arriving through the same feed with the same visual weight. He treats sources as interchangeable because *his whole informational life* has treated them as interchangeable. This is not a character flaw and the story refuses to frame it as one. It is a faithful description of how a 6th grader on a phone in 2026 encounters information.

His weakness is `uncritical_acceptance`. In episode 1 he cites a TikTok about the squirrels as if it were equivalent to a news article, and nobody initially pushes back because his tone is confident and the clip is interesting. His growth is not "stop using the phone" — the story would be dishonest to expect that — but learning that *the phone is a pile of sources, not a source*, and that asking where something came from is a move you can make without being the kid who slows everything down.

Cam's growth runs across two beats. In episode 2 — small, begrudging — he comes back having looked up the creator of his TikTok source and found out they were a college kid running a comedy account, and he reports this with some visible annoyance because it was his mom who pushed him to check. In episode 6 — the real beat — he asks his mom's provenance question ("who made that, and what do they get out of it being true?") about *his own* source, unprompted, and he visibly notices himself doing it. That second beat is the confirmation that his growth is real and not externally driven.

In episode 2 specifically, Cam also exhibits `overgeneralization` as a secondary cognitive pattern: after counting three white squirrels in a single afternoon walking the south side of the park, he concludes *they are everywhere now*. That move produces a weak `sufficiency` value in the same episode — three sightings on one walk is not a lot, and the conclusion runs past what the sample can support. The overgeneralization is not his primary weakness (that remains `uncritical_acceptance` of source provenance), but it surfaces cleanly in the count episode and pairs with Jules's episode-1 sufficiency weakness — one sighting → whole hypothesis — to give the Evidence lens two distinct thin-evidence instances through two different cognitive patterns before Maya's Logic arc takes over.

Cam's carried facets are `source_credibility`, `source_diversity`, and `sufficiency` (the last specifically in episode 2), appearing as weaknesses in episodes 1 and 2 and as growing strengths in episodes 6 and 8. His second growth beat is slightly overcorrecting — in episode 6 he refuses to cite anything before checking provenance, and it slows the group down and is a little annoying, because new skills are clumsy. Lens: Evidence.

### Priya

Priya is the Scope grower and her weakness is the subtlest in the cast. She lives in East Memphis, near Audubon Park. Both of her parents are University of Memphis faculty — her mom in biology (a real lever for the story, since she is how the group gets in to see the zookeeper), her dad in economics. At the start of the season Priya is *already* good at the Logic and Evidence lenses. She brings real sources. Her arguments hold up. She is, in the group's mental picture, the one who knows things.

Her weakness is `egocentric_thinking` in the framework's exact sense — not selfish, *epistemically local*. Priya reasons from the Memphis she knows and does not notice that there is other Memphis. When she says "everyone's dad just Googles things" she means her dad; her dad is an economist; she has never considered that the sentence might not generalize. Her weakness is insulation, not arrogance, and it is going to be the hardest weakness for 6th grade readers to see. The story compensates by making her inflection the most *physical* of any character's: she has to literally walk somewhere she has never walked before she can see what she has been missing.

Priya's first visible weak move is in episode 3, at the zoo. While the group is talking with the zookeeper she says something that generalizes from her own Memphis to *everyone's* Memphis — the exact phrasing is the dialog writer's to pick, but the shape is something like *"everyone's parents have someone they can just text to look stuff up"* or *"we can always ask a professor, that's what my mom does"* — and the group lets it pass without pushback. That sentence is the on-page anchor for her scope weakness. It is not her inflection; the inflection is the north-side walk in episode 6. But it gives the planning agent and the dialog writer a concrete move to encode, so the weakness has a visible trace and not only a structural absence.

Priya's growth is then set up by her *absence* in episode 5. The group is plotting sightings on a map of the park and the scope problem — they have only been walking the south side — is visible to the reader, but none of the three present kids can quite name it. The one person in the group who would have caught it is Priya, and she isn't there, and the reason she isn't there is part of what she has to learn. In episode 6 the group walks the north side of Overton Park and Priya names the sampling bias out loud: "we were drawing a map of the part of the park we walk." That is her inflection, and it is as much about her map of Memphis as about the park.

Priya's carried facets are `condition_sensitivity` and `perspective_engagement`. Her cognitive pattern is `egocentric_thinking`. The story also uses her briefly in episode 3 as a lever for a one-beat group-access texture: her mom's University of Memphis connection is how the kids get in to see the zookeeper, and the group notices the access once and lets it pass. It is not a recurring thread. Lens: Scope.

**Scaffolding note for downstream design.** Priya's weakness is the subtlest in the cast and it will require the heaviest hint support of the four growers. `scaffolding_id` should treat her arc as the highest-risk calibration target in the story. Episodes 3 and 5 are where the scaffolding has to do its work: episode 3 because Priya's only visible weak move of the early season lives there (the Memphis-generalization sentence), and episode 5 because the scope problem is present on the page but nobody in the scene names it. Hints for both episodes will need to direct attention toward "whose Memphis is missing" and "whose walk is missing" without naming `perspective_engagement` or `condition_sensitivity` outright. Miscalibrated hints risk either giving the observation away or missing it entirely. Pilot this one with care.

### Tye

Tye is the anchor. He lives in Frayser and has an older sister in 11th grade at Central High who taught him, years ago, to write his questions down before trying to answer them. Tye does not have a growth arc. He is stable across the season, and his stability is not passivity — it is the scaffolding that lets four growth arcs happen in parallel without the group falling apart. He is the kid who, in every episode, at some point asks *wait — what were we originally trying to figure out?*, and he is the kid who keeps the notebook.

Tye's stable strength is `reasoning_completeness`. He does not reason more cleverly than the other four. He reasons *more completely*, because he writes things down and reads them back. His carried facets show up as strengths in multiple episodes rather than as growth beats, and his cognitive and social profile is deliberately light — he is not a weakness carrier for any pattern or dynamic. The story's architecture requires this: with four growth arcs in motion, the reader needs at least one fixed point to orient around.

Tye's background surfaces through a recurring texture rather than a scene. Across the season he says "my sister says" half a dozen times, starting as throwaway and accumulating into a real portrait by episode 8, at which point the group realizes his whole investigative style is inherited. His sister is never on-screen and never will be. She is a voice inside his head.

Tye is tested once, lightly, in episode 5. The group has plotted sightings on a map and the south-side data looks consistent and Maya's episode-4 inferential chain fits it, and for a beat the group is tempted to call the mystery solved and close the notebook. Tye is the one tempted — he has been the notebook-keeper all season and the idea of writing the answer on the last page is appealing. He resists by doing the thing his sister taught him to do: he rereads the original question from episode 1 out loud, and the rereading forces him to notice that the question was broader than the answer, and he keeps the notebook open. That beat is his stability-tested-and-holds moment, and it is the reason he reads as a character and not as a function in a story with four other arcs running.

## Arc summary

The eight episodes trace a single investigation from thin hypothesis to honest answer. Episode 1 is the sighting and the first weak claims — Jules proposes aliens on the basis of one white squirrel (the season's purest sufficiency weakness, paired with her `false_cause` chain), Cam cites a TikTok, Tye writes the question down. Episode 2 is the squirrel count, where Cam earns his first small evidence-audit beat *and* exhibits `overgeneralization` as a secondary pattern when three sightings on one walk become *they're everywhere*. Episode 3 is the zookeeper scene, where the group meets genotype and phenotype as concepts, where Jules's `authority_deference` surfaces as the echo of her `uncritical_acceptance` of her mother, where Priya makes her visible scope-weakness move (generalizing from her own Memphis) and the group lets it pass, and where Maya's Logic weakness gets fully planted — she can feel the chain is right and cannot reconstruct it. **Downstream scaffolding note:** episode 3 asks its scaffolding to do double duty — it has to teach the genotype/phenotype concept well enough that episode 7's probability reasoning and episode 8's falsifiable reframe still land, *and* it has to teach the Logic-lens reconstruction move that Maya is failing to make. `scaffolding_id` should treat this episode as the highest-load scaffolding episode in the season. Episode 4 is the notebook, where Maya writes the chain out step by step for the first time in her life and catches her own bad inference mid-writing. Episode 5 is the map, which the group plots without Priya. The map is a physical object but the transcript is dialogue-only, so it has to surface through kids reading coordinates aloud, tracing paths out loud (*"that's the third one, by the shelter"*), and naming which paths they walked — the scope problem sits in the shape of what gets read out, because every sighting is clustered along the paths the group actually walks. Tye is tempted to close the notebook and resists by rereading the original question from episode 1. Episode 6 is dual-purpose: it is Scope-primary for Priya's inflection when the group walks the north side, and it also carries Cam's second unprompted Evidence beat — his provenance-checking move is now his own and not his mother's. The two beats are designed to land in sequence (Priya's inflection as the group walks, Cam's confirmation at the picnic table afterwards) rather than compete for the same turns. Episode 7 is the numbers, where Maya constructs a second inferential chain — on base rates and founder effects — faster and cleaner than the first, confirming her growth is real, and where Jules's `authority_deference` makes its second appearance as she cites the zookeeper as settled truth and Cam pushes back. Episode 8 is the reframe, where Jules formulates her alien hypothesis falsifiably and concedes honestly, Tye reads the original question from episode 1 back to the group, and they check their answer against it.

Across the eight episodes the lens primacy rotates. Episodes 1 and 2 are Evidence-primary. Episodes 3, 4, and 7 are Logic-primary. Episodes 5 and 6 are Scope-primary. Episode 8 is Logic-primary (Jules's falsifiable reformulation of her own hypothesis is a move on `inferential_validity`) and functions as a season-level synthesis beat. No lens dominates; Logic carries three because Maya's growth is the most demanding and needs three beats to earn.

The mixed-valence shapes rotate across all five of the allowed shapes. Episode 1 is an early-strength-collapse: Tye writes the original question down and neither Jules nor Cam engages with what he has written. Episode 2 is a small self-correction (Cam's first beat). Episode 3 is unresolved disagreement, with the Logic problem now named but unsolved. Episode 4 is strength-prevails on Maya's side, paired with a scope weakness the group cannot yet see. Episode 5 is a stalemate — the answer is on the table and nobody notices, and the episode closes without anyone naming it. Episode 6 is distributed self-correction. Episode 7 is strength-prevails (Maya's, confirmed). Episode 8 is strength-prevails at the season level. All five shapes appear; no shape carries more than three episodes.

## Stakes

What the kids stand to learn is the real subject. There is nothing concrete to lose in this story — the park isn't closing, the squirrels aren't endangered, no adult is threatening anything. What is at stake is whether Jules can be right the way her mother would want her to be right, whether Maya can show her work the way her father would want her to show it, whether Cam can learn what his mother already knows about sources, whether Priya can see the Memphis she has been invisible to, and whether the five of them can arrive at a real answer by doing real reasoning instead of by being already smart.

The answer the kids arrive at is correct: the white squirrels in Overton Park are almost certainly recessive homozygotes in a founder-limited urban population, and the math works out. But they arrive at it on a thinner thread than a real biologist would tolerate, and the story's final judgment is on the quality of the reasoning path, not on whether they were right.

## Pedagogical commitments

This story is built around four things students should walk away noticing.

**One: thin reasoning is not wrong reasoning — it is reasoning that hasn't finished yet.** Every character in the cast starts the season with a genuinely weak move, and the story refuses to treat those weak moves as failures of character. Jules's alien hypothesis is earned from her family. Maya's feel-first reasoning is earned from a household full of arguments that worked by feel. Cam's interchangeable sources are earned from the information environment he actually lives in. Priya's local reasoning is earned from a life spent in one part of the city. The right response to any of these is not "you're wrong" but "show me more," and the growth beats are structured as *doing more work*, not as *admitting fault*.

**Two: growth is earned on screen, not narrated.** A character saying "I learned my lesson" is cheap. A character in episode 4 doing a thing they visibly could not do in episode 3, without anyone prompting them, is the real thing. Each of the three lens-growers (Maya, Cam, Priya) has a specific on-screen move that marks their inflection — Maya writing the chain, Cam asking the provenance question unprompted, Priya walking the north side of the park — and each move is set up by an earlier episode where the same character failed to make the move. Students watching the season should be able to point at both ends of each arc.

**Three: the visible layer is the tip of a hidden layer.** Genotype and phenotype are the story's conceptual payoff and they rhyme with the Polylogue framework's own architecture — visible moves in a conversation are the surface expression of hidden reasoning, exactly as white squirrels in Overton Park are the visible expression of a recessive allele that has been carried silently by every grey squirrel in the population for generations. A reader who notices this resonance has understood something about reasoning, not just something about squirrels.

**Four: good reasoning produces honest answers, not confident ones.** The season's final episode does not end with a triumphant announcement. It ends with Tye reading the original question from episode 1 back to the group, the group checking their answer against it, and Jules stating on the record that aliens *would predict* one thing and genetics predict another, and the data fits genetics. Nobody claims certainty. The answer is right and the thread is thinner than anyone would like, and saying so is the most grown-up move in the story.

**A focused-coverage trade-off worth naming.** This story declares `authority_deference` as its only social dynamic. That is a deliberate choice for *this* story: it teaches the *authority texture* of reasoning — how confident voices (a parent, a brother, a zookeeper) get accepted without their reasoning being reconstructed — but it does not teach the *peer-pressure* or *conflict-avoidance* textures that `group_pressure` and `conflict_avoidance` would carry. These five kids argue with each other freely, and putting peer-pressure dynamics on a cast whose whole premise is honest disagreement would fight the story. The absence of the other two dynamics here is a focused-coverage commitment for *The Overton Park Sightings* specifically — it is not a promise about how other stories will divide the labor. Downstream reviewers and facilitators should treat the absence as intentional within this story, not as a gap to patch.
